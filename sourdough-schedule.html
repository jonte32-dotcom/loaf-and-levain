How this calculator works.

Sourdough timing is governed by enzyme activity and yeast metabolism, both of which scale predictably with temperature. As a rule of thumb fermentation rate roughly doubles for every 8°C rise in dough temperature. The calculator uses that relationship, along with your inoculation percentage, to estimate bulk fermentation duration, then schedules the rest of the steps around it.

Temperature drives everything

Most kitchen sourdough disasters trace back to a single mistake: ignoring room temperature. A dough that finishes bulk in five hours at 26°C will need eight to ten at 20°C. Dough fully proofed at midnight in summer will be barely awake at midnight in winter. Measuring actual room temperature, not the thermostat, makes the difference between consistent loaves and a year of frustration.

Inoculation as a timing dial

The percentage of starter relative to total flour is the second knob you have. More starter (25–30%) accelerates bulk; less (10–15%) slows it. Bakers prefer low inoculation in summer to keep timelines manageable, and bump it up in winter when ambient temperatures drag everything out.

Cold retard isn't optional, but it is flexible

Twelve hours at 4°C develops flavor, makes scoring easier, and lets you bake in the morning instead of after midnight. Longer retards (24–36 hours) intensify tang. Skip retard entirely and you can bake same-day, but expect a tighter window between proof and overproof.

Starter readiness, decoded

Active and peaked: at least doubled, dome on top, floats in water — use within an hour of peak. Recently fed: needs four to six more hours to ripen at room temperature. Cold from fridge: warm up, feed, wait for peak — total around eight to twelve hours, sometimes split across two feeds for vigor. The Starter tab handles this math automatically.

The DDT formula

Desired Dough Temperature is a professional baker's tool for landing dough at a target temperature immediately after mixing. The formula is simple: target × 4 minus the sum of flour temp, room temp, and friction (estimated 2–4°C for hand mixing, 5–8°C for stand mixers). Solve for water temp. The Recipe Lab does this in real time so you can stop guessing whether your kitchen is warm enough.

Bulk fermentation by temperature: a baker's guide

Bulk fermentation is the part of a sourdough bake where most outcomes are decided. Get the timing right and you get an open crumb, a clean ear, and dough that's a pleasure to shape. Get it wrong and the rest of the schedule cannot save you. The single biggest variable that decides bulk timing isn't your starter, your flour, or your inoculation — it's your kitchen temperature.

The Q10 rule, in plain words

Yeast and lactic bacteria work faster when it's warm and slower when it's cold. The rate roughly doubles for every 8°C (about 14°F) rise in dough temperature. Bakers call this Q10 ≈ 2.

What this means in practice:

  • At 24°C (75°F) a 20% inoculation white-flour dough finishes bulk in about 5 hours.
  • At 20°C (68°F) the same dough takes about 7.5 hours.
  • At 16°C (61°F) it takes nearly 11 hours.
  • At 28°C (82°F) it's done in about 3 hours and 20 minutes.

The dough you mixed at 9 a.m. on a 22°C summer Saturday will be ready around 3 p.m. The same dough mixed at 9 a.m. on a 17°C winter Saturday will be ready around 7 p.m. Treating those two days as if they were the same bake is the most common reason home loaves come out underproofed in winter and overproofed in summer.

Measure dough temperature, not air temperature

Dough is usually warmer than the room. Friction from mixing adds 1–4°C, and the dough then loses heat slowly because flour and water hold heat well. If your kitchen is 22°C, your dough may be sitting at 24–25°C right after mixing. Plan for the dough's temperature, not the thermostat.

A cheap instant-read probe thermometer is enough. Stick it in the centre of the dough right after mixing. That number is what you should feed into the schedule calculator.

Inoculation as a second dial

After temperature, inoculation (the percentage of starter relative to total flour) is your other lever. More starter speeds bulk down; less starter slows it.

  • 10–15% inoculation — slower bulk, often 8–14 hours at room temp. Good for summer or for letting bulk run overnight in winter.
  • 20% inoculation — the most common starting point. Predictable.
  • 25–30% inoculation — faster bulk, sometimes 3 hours or less in a warm kitchen. Useful for same-day bakes.

The relationship is roughly linear. Halving the inoculation roughly doubles the bulk time. Doubling the inoculation roughly halves it.

Visual cues beat the clock

A schedule is a planning tool, not a stopwatch. The dough finishes bulk when it does, not when the timer says. Look for:

  • 50–75% rise in volume from the moment salt was added. Mark the side of the bowl with a wax pencil or a piece of tape so you can see this clearly.
  • A domed top that holds its shape when you tilt the bowl gently.
  • A jiggle — when you wobble the container, the surface moves like a thick custard, not like wet concrete.
  • Bubbles visible at the surface and the sides of the container.

If two of those four are present, you're at or near the end of bulk.

How to use the calculator with your kitchen

Open the schedule calculator, set inoculation, hydration, and salt to your recipe. Then set the room-temperature slider to your measured room temperature, not the value on your thermostat. The calculator uses the Q10 model above to estimate bulk duration, then schedules everything else around it — autolyse, folds, pre-shape, retard, and bake-out.

If you find your bulk consistently runs faster than predicted, your dough is probably warmer than the room (heating from the floor, sun on the counter, oven lights, etc.). Drop the room slider 1–2°C and the prediction will line up.

What changes the model can't see

The calculator assumes a healthy starter peaking on schedule. If your starter is sluggish, off-schedule, or over-fed, no temperature math fixes that — it adds latency at the front. The Starter tab in the same tool plans those feeds backwards from mix time so your levain actually peaks when you need it.

The model also assumes white bread flour. Whole-grain flours ferment faster — they bring more enzymes and more wild yeast on the bran. If you're running 50% whole wheat, expect to shave 20–30% off the predicted bulk time, and watch the dough closely.

A starting recipe for calibration

If you've never measured before, run this once: 500 g bread flour, 75% hydration (375 g water), 20% inoculation (100 g starter), 2% salt (10 g). Mix at a known time. Note the dough temperature. Bulk in a clear container with a marked starting line. Note the time when the dough hits the 65% rise mark.

That single observation calibrates the model to your kitchen forever. You'll find your bulks are within ±20 minutes of the predicted value from then on, regardless of season — as long as you measure dough temperature, not room temperature.

Why is my sourdough gummy inside (and how to fix it)

You cut into the loaf, the crust crackles, the crumb looks beautiful — and then the bottom centimetre is wet, sticky, almost paste-like. That's gummy crumb. It's the most common sourdough failure mode, and the reason isn't always the one bakers expect.

What "gummy" actually means

Gummy crumb is dough that hasn't fully transitioned from gel to bread. The starches haven't fully gelatinised, or they have but the structure trapping the steam wasn't strong enough to hold gas through the bake. The result is a damp, dense, doughy strip — usually at the bottom of the loaf where heat takes longest to penetrate.

It is not a hydration problem. Bread at 85% hydration can have a perfect open crumb. Bread at 70% hydration can be gummy. Hydration is rarely the cause.

Cause #1: underproofed dough (the most common)

If your bulk fermentation was too short, the dough enters the oven without enough gas in the structure. It overheats from the outside in before the centre can bake. The crust forms, the crumb behind it doesn't, and you get gummy texture.

How to tell: a dense, tight crumb with little open structure. Few or no large alveoli. The loaf may look pale on top.

How to fix: extend bulk by 1–2 hours, or raise the inoculation by 5%. Track rise visually — aim for 65–75% volume increase from the moment salt was added, not from the start of mixing. The bulk fermentation calculator can predict the right window for your kitchen temperature.

Cause #2: overproofed dough (common in summer)

Counterintuitively, dough that's too fermented also bakes gummy. When bulk runs long, the gluten structure breaks down, the dough can't hold gas, and oven spring fails. The loaf flattens during baking, leaving a dense, sticky crumb.

How to tell: a flat loaf with little or no oven spring. Big bubbles right under the crust ("flying crust") and dense gummy crumb below them.

How to fix: shorten bulk by 1–2 hours, drop inoculation 5%, or move the dough to a cooler spot during bulk. In summer, watch the kitchen temperature — every 8°C above 24°C cuts bulk roughly in half.

Cause #3: cut too early (zero-effort fix)

A loaf cut while still warm reads as gummy because steam hasn't escaped the crumb. The inside is still hot enough that water is condensed in the crumb structure. Wait at least an hour after the loaf comes out of the oven — the same loaf, cut at room temperature, will read as soft and bouncy instead of gummy.

This is the easiest fix in baking. Set a timer. Walk away.

Cause #4: underbaked (verify with a thermometer)

A loaf that comes out at the visual sign of doneness (deep colour, hollow knock) is sometimes still underbaked internally. Sourdough crumb needs an internal temperature of 96–99°C (205–210°F) to be fully set.

If you've never measured, do this once: stick an instant-read thermometer through the bottom of the loaf into the centre when you take it out. If it reads under 96°C, give it 5–10 more minutes in the oven, lid off, and use that data to calibrate your bake going forward.

Cause #5: insufficient steam (less common)

If the crust sets too early, the loaf can't expand and the inside compacts. This is rare with a Dutch oven (which traps its own steam) but happens often with open baking on a stone if no steam source is provided. Symptoms: pale, soft, almost matte crust with dense gummy interior.

Fix: bake covered for the first 20 minutes (Dutch oven lid on, or a roasting pan inverted over the loaf), then uncover for browning.

Cause #6: the recipe is wrong for the flour

Whole-grain flours absorb more water and ferment faster. If you took a 75% hydration recipe written for bread flour and made it with 50% whole wheat at the same hydration, you'll often get gummy results because there isn't enough water to fully hydrate the bran, and the bulk runs faster than the schedule predicts.

For 30%+ whole grain, raise hydration by 5–8% and shorten bulk by 15–20%.

A diagnostic checklist

Before changing your recipe, run through this list in order:

  1. Did you cut into it warm? If yes, retest with a fully cooled loaf next time.
  2. What was the internal temperature at bake-out? If under 96°C, bake longer.
  3. What did the bulk look like — was the dough dense and tight, or flat and big-bubbled? Underproofed vs overproofed have different fixes.
  4. What was your room temperature during bulk? Plug it into the schedule calculator and compare predicted vs actual bulk duration.
  5. Did you bake covered? If not, try a Dutch oven for 20 minutes covered then 25 uncovered.
  6. What flour did you use? Whole-grain blends need their own recipe, not white-flour numbers with WW substituted.

Most gummy loaves trace back to causes 1, 2, or 3. Fix those first before chasing exotic explanations.

When to change one variable at a time

The temptation after a gummy loaf is to change three things at once: longer bulk, higher temperature, more steam. Don't. Change one variable, bake again, and see what moved. You'll learn faster, and you'll have a stable recipe in 3–4 bakes instead of 15.

How to revive a forgotten sourdough starter

You opened the fridge and there it is — your starter. Last fed three weeks ago. Maybe two months ago. The hooch is dark, there's a sour smell, possibly a thin grey skin on top. The good news: unless there's actual mould (fuzzy, coloured spots), it's recoverable. Wild yeast and lactic bacteria are surprisingly hard to kill. They go dormant, not dead.

Here's how to bring it back, and how to know when it's ready to bake with again.

First, decide if it's dead or just sleeping

Probably alive (recoverable):

  • Dark liquid (hooch) on top — that's just alcohol from fermentation
  • Strong vinegar or acetone smell
  • Grey or brownish skin on top
  • No bubbles visible

Probably dead or contaminated (start over):

  • Pink, orange, green, or fuzzy growth — that's mould
  • Bright pink streaks — that's bacterial contamination
  • Smell of rotting garbage or sweaty socks (not just sour or alcoholic)

If you see any of the second list, throw it out and start fresh. A new starter is 7 days of effort and there's no point fighting contamination.

The 3-feed revival protocol

Most fridge-forgotten starters need three feeds at room temperature to come fully back. Here's the sequence:

Feed 1: the wake-up

Pour off the hooch. Scrape out a small amount — about 10 g — of the surviving starter from underneath the surface. Discard the rest. The reason: you don't want to dilute weak yeast with a huge amount of fresh flour and water; you want to concentrate what's alive.

Mix that 10 g with 10 g flour and 10 g water (a 1:1:1 ratio). Cover loosely. Leave it at room temperature for 8–12 hours.

You probably won't see much. Maybe a few bubbles, maybe nothing. That's normal. The yeast is rebuilding population.

Feed 2: the build

After 8–12 hours, your wake-up feed should at least show some bubbles. Take 10 g of it, add 20 g flour and 20 g water (1:2:2 ratio). Cover. Leave at room temperature.

This feed should show clear life within 6–8 hours at 22°C — visible doming, bubbles on the side, a clean fermented smell instead of sharp vinegar.

If nothing happens after 12 hours, repeat Feed 2. Some starters need an extra cycle.

Feed 3: the final build

When Feed 2 has clearly doubled and is at peak, do one more feed at the ratio you'll bake with. For most home bakers that's 1:2:2 again. Take 20 g, add 40 g flour and 40 g water. Wait for peak — usually 5–6 hours at 22°C.

A peaked starter looks like this: domed top, full of bubbles, smells like beer and yogurt, and floats in water (the float test). This is the starter you bake with.

Use the starter calculator to plan backwards

The Starter tab in this calculator handles the math for you. Tell it when you need the starter ready, your kitchen temperature, and how many feeds you want (3 for a fridge-forgotten starter), and it gives you the exact times and amounts for each feed.

For example: if you want a peaked starter Saturday at 18:00 to mix dough, and your kitchen is 22°C, the calculator might tell you: Friday 17:00 wake-up feed (10/10/10), Saturday 04:00 build feed (10/20/20), Saturday 12:00 final feed (20/40/40), peaks 18:00. That's not something most bakers want to do in their head — let the tool do it.

Common revival problems and what they mean

Acetone smell, no rise. The starter is starving. It's eaten all the available sugars and produced alcohol as a stress response. Discard most of it and feed at 1:5:5 (a strong feed) — give it lots of food.

Bubbles but no rise. Yeast is alive but population is low. Feed at 1:1:1 (a tight feed) for two cycles to concentrate it, then build up.

Big rise, then total collapse and watery surface. Starter is over-fermented — it peaked and crashed before you got to it. Keep feeding at the same ratio but catch it earlier next cycle. The starter isn't broken; you're just timing the next feed wrong.

Smells like nail polish. Acetone again. Same fix as above — feed it more, more often.

Starter peaks, then doesn't rise next feed. This usually means the previous batch wasn't actually mature, or the room temperature changed. Don't panic. Feed it at the same ratio, give it more time, and re-check.

The maintenance schedule that prevents this

Once your starter is healthy again, you have two options to avoid forgetting it:

Option A: bake weekly. Feed Friday evening, mix dough Saturday afternoon, bake Sunday morning. Leftover starter goes back in the fridge for a week. This is the simplest schedule and keeps a starter happy.

Option B: bake monthly. Refresh the starter once a week — pull from fridge, do one feed at room temp until peaked, put back. This adds 10 minutes a week and keeps the starter ready for an unplanned bake.

The trap is keeping a starter "for emergencies" without ever baking. Yeast needs to eat. A starter fed once every 6 weeks isn't being maintained — it's slowly dying. If you don't bake regularly, give the starter to a friend who does, and start a fresh one when you're ready to commit again.

When to give up and start over

After three full revival cycles (three rounds of all three feeds), if the starter still won't peak reliably or doubles within 8 hours at 22°C, it's time to start fresh. Whole-rye flour and water for 5–7 days will give you a working starter. The fork in the road is rarely worth more than 24 hours of revival effort.

Sourdough hydration explained: 65%, 75%, 85%, 100%

Hydration is the percentage of water in your dough relative to flour. A 75% hydration dough has 75 g of water for every 100 g of flour. That's it — the math is simple. What's not simple is how dramatically different doughs behave at different hydrations, and why you'd choose one over another.

This is the practical guide to picking a hydration that actually matches your skill, your flour, and the bread you want.

How to read a baker's percentage

Hydration is always calculated against total flour weight, so:

  • 500 g flour at 75% hydration = 375 g water
  • 1000 g flour at 80% hydration = 800 g water
  • 300 g flour at 100% hydration = 300 g water (equal weights)

The starter (levain) is usually 100% hydration — equal flour and water — so a recipe with 20% inoculation contributes some additional flour and water that strict bakers count separately. For home baking, the difference is small enough that you can ignore it unless you're chasing competition-level precision.

What changes as hydration goes up

Higher hydration = more open crumb, glossier interior, better fermentation activity. But also: stickier handling, weaker shape, harder scoring, more skill required.

Here's what each band looks like in practice.

65–70% — beginner-friendly territory

A 65% dough feels like clay. It's easy to handle, holds shape well, and forgives a lot of mistakes. The crumb will be tight by sourdough standards — small even alveoli, almost like a sandwich loaf. The crust will be crisp but the interior will be on the dense side.

Use this hydration if:

  • You're in your first 10 sourdough bakes
  • You want sandwich bread
  • You're using a flour with low protein (cake flour, all-purpose under 11% protein)
  • You're working with a lot of whole grain (which absorbs water differently)

Recipes: pain de mie, sandwich loaves, brioche-style sourdough, very dense rye breads.

70–78% — the sweet spot

This is where most published sourdough recipes live. The dough is still manageable — sticky enough that you need wet hands for stretch and folds, but firm enough to hold a strong shape. Crumb is open without being chaotic. Scoring is straightforward.

A 75% hydration country loaf with bread flour is the most-baked sourdough recipe in the world for a reason: it works. Mistakes are forgiven. Results are predictable.

Use this hydration if:

  • You're in your first 30 bakes
  • You want a classic country loaf appearance
  • You're using bread flour (12.5–13.5% protein)
  • You bake in a Dutch oven

Recipes: country loaf, multigrain, mostly-white sourdough.

78–85% — the open-crumb push

Now things get interesting. At 80%+ the dough acts more like a wet batter for the first hour, then develops surprising strength as gluten organises during folds. Final shaping is harder — you need confidence and a quick hand. The crumb gets dramatically more open: large irregular alveoli, glossy walls.

Use this hydration if:

  • You've bought a bench scraper and you actually use it
  • You can do a coil fold without dragging the dough
  • You're using strong bread flour (13%+ protein) or T80
  • You want that "open crumb" look you see on Instagram

Recipes: high-hydration country loaf, ciabatta, focaccia.

85–95% — advanced territory

These doughs flow. They look like they'll never become bread, then they do, and the result is glossy and full of character. Mistakes punish you — overworked dough turns soupy, underworked dough won't shape, and timing windows are tight.

Use this hydration if:

  • You've baked 50+ loaves
  • You can read the dough by feel
  • You have strong bread flour
  • You're after specific rustic styles

Recipes: pan de cristal, very open ciabatta, certain Spanish-style loaves.

100%+ — pan de cristal territory

At equal-weight water and flour, you're not making a shaped loaf anymore — you're making a slab. The dough is poured rather than shaped. The crumb is dramatic: nearly all hole, very thin walls, a glassy interior. These are showcase loaves, not weekday loaves.

The flour question

Hydration doesn't exist independently of flour. The same 80% recipe will be sloppy with 11% protein flour and perfectly handled with 13% protein flour. Whole-grain flours absorb 5–10% more water than white flour because of the bran's surface area. Old flour absorbs less than fresh flour.

Practical translation:

  • All-purpose (10–11.5% protein): stay at 65–72%
  • Bread flour (12–13.5%): comfortable up to 78%, possible to 82% with technique
  • Strong bread flour / T80 (13–14%): comfortable up to 85%, possible higher
  • Whole wheat (any): add 5–8% to whatever the recipe says
  • Rye (significant percentage): different rules entirely — see the rye guide

Use the calculator to convert

If you find a 75% recipe online but your flour can really only handle 70%, the recipe lab calculator recalculates everything proportionally. Drop hydration to 70% and the water grams update, salt and starter stay at their target percentages, and the total dough weight adjusts. Same for scaling — turn a 500 g recipe into 800 g without doing math.

Don't chase hydration as a goal

Higher hydration is not better bread. It's a different bread, and it requires more from you. A confidently made 72% loaf with a clean ear and consistent crumb beats a sloppy 85% that flattened during retard and produced an irregular mess. Pick the hydration that matches the bread you want and the skill you have. Push it up only when you've mastered the level below.

Cold retard vs same-day bake: when to use each

The cold retard — that 12 to 36-hour rest in the fridge between final shaping and baking — is one of the most-asked-about steps in sourdough. Some bakers swear it's essential. Others skip it entirely. Both groups make great bread. The truth is that retard is a tool, not a rule, and knowing when to use it is more useful than knowing how long it should be.

What cold retard actually does

When dough goes from 22°C to 4°C, fermentation slows by about 10× — but it doesn't stop. Yeast produces less CO2; lactic and acetic acid bacteria keep working at slightly different rates than they did at room temperature, shifting the flavour balance. Meanwhile, the gluten relaxes, the dough firms up, and the surface dries slightly.

The four practical effects:

  1. Flavour develops. Longer fermentation, especially the bacterial side of it, produces more acid and more flavour compounds. A 24-hour retard tastes more sour and more complex than a 12-hour retard.
  2. Scoring gets clean. Cold dough holds blade marks crisply where warm dough drags and tears.
  3. Schedules shift. You can mix Saturday morning and bake Sunday morning instead of baking at midnight Saturday.
  4. The crust browns better. More acid means the crust caramelises faster in the oven, giving deeper colour and more flavour.

When same-day bake is the right call

Skip the retard when:

  • You want the bread today. Same-day means 6–9 hours total instead of 24–36.
  • It's a high-hydration dough you're worried about. Some very wet doughs flatten during long retards. A 1–2 hour final proof at room temp before baking gives them less time to spread.
  • You're baking ciabatta, focaccia, or pan de cristal. These styles benefit from an active fermentation right up to the oven. Cold retard works for them but isn't traditional.
  • You're using a very ripe levain in summer. A short, hot bulk and a brief proof give a bright, mildly sour bread that some people prefer to the deeper sour of long retard.

The same-day bake schedule generated by the calculator (set retard to 0) plans a final proof of 1–2 hours at room temperature after final shaping, then straight to the oven. Total elapsed time from feeding the starter to slicing the bread is around 8–10 hours.

When retard is worth the wait

Use a 12–24 hour retard when:

  • You want to bake in the morning instead of late at night. This is the original reason home bakers retard.
  • You want a more sour, more complex flavour profile. 24+ hours develops noticeable sourness even in mild starters.
  • You want the easiest scoring of your life. Cold dough scores like clay. There's no comparison.
  • You're baking on a deadline. A retarded loaf can sit in the fridge for 12–36 hours, giving you a window to bake when convenient instead of when the dough demands.
  • You want maximum oven spring. Cold dough hits the hot Dutch oven and the temperature differential drives a massive expansion. Same-day loaves are bigger; retarded loaves are taller.

A 12-hour retard is the home-baker default for a reason: it's the minimum time that delivers all the benefits without the dough deteriorating.

Beyond 24 hours: diminishing returns and risks

Retards longer than 24 hours give incrementally more flavour, but you start to face two risks:

Over-acidification. The dough becomes so sour the flavour goes from "complex" to "sharp". Some bakers love this; others find it dominates the bread.

Structural breakdown. After 36 hours at 4°C, gluten starts to weaken from prolonged enzyme activity. The dough becomes harder to score (it tears) and flattens during baking.

For most home bakers, 12–24 hours is the useful range. 36–48 hours is for specific results — very sour breads, specific climate-driven schedules — not as a default.

How to tell when a retarded dough is ready

A retarded dough doesn't need to "warm up" before baking, contrary to common advice. Take it straight from the fridge, score it, drop it in the hot Dutch oven, lid on. The temperature shock is what drives oven spring.

To check that it's actually proofed enough before retard, do the poke test on the shaped loaf as you put it in the fridge: press a floured finger 5 mm into the dough. The indent should spring back slowly, leaving a slight depression. If it springs back fully, it needs more proof at room temp before retard. If it doesn't spring back at all, you've over-proofed and should bake now without retard.

Mixing schedules around retard length

The schedule calculator handles all of this. Set bake-out time to your target (e.g., Sunday 09:00), set retard to 12, 16, 24, or 36 hours, and the calculator works backwards: it tells you when to mix, when to do each fold, when to pre-shape, when to put the dough in the fridge.

This is the difference between cooking and timing. A bake that ends at 09:00 with a 24-hour retard requires you to start mixing the previous day around 14:00 — but the actual labour is maybe 30 minutes spread across that window. The retard does the rest.

A simple decision framework

  • First sourdough bake? 12-hour retard. It's forgiving and the schedule is reasonable.
  • Want bread tonight? Skip retard.
  • Want bread in the morning? 12-hour retard.
  • Want maximum flavour and you have time? 24-hour retard.
  • Working around a busy schedule? Long retard buys you flexibility.
  • Trying to compete with a bakery loaf? 18–24 hour retard. The flavour difference is real.

The retard isn't a step to memorise — it's a planning tool. Use it when it serves the bake. Skip it when it doesn't.

The float test: when is your starter actually ready?

Drop a teaspoon of starter into a glass of water. If it floats, your starter is ready to bake. If it sinks, wait. That's the float test in one sentence — and it's the most-cited starter readiness test online. It's also more nuanced than it looks. Here's when it works, when it lies, and what to do when you can't trust it.

Why the float test works (most of the time)

A starter at peak is full of CO2 trapped in a gluten-protein network. The bubbles make it less dense than water, so a small piece floats. When the starter has crashed past peak, it's lost most of its gas — the bubbles burst and the dough sags. Now it's denser than water, and it sinks.

So a positive float test usually means: "this starter has enough yeast activity to leaven bread." A negative test usually means: "this starter is either pre-peak (not ready yet) or post-peak (already crashed)."

Note the word "usually." The test has failure modes.

When the float test gives a false positive

A starter can float and still be a poor candidate for baking:

  • Cold starter. A starter pulled straight from the fridge often floats — the cold thickens it and CO2 from previous activity is still trapped. This doesn't mean it's strong enough to leaven your dough.
  • Whole-rye starter. Rye creates such viscous, gummy starter that even crashed rye can float. Don't use float test on rye-only starters.
  • Very stiff starter. A 50% hydration starter (more flour than water) is dense and gas-trapping. It can float for hours after crashing.
  • Recent feed at high ratio. A 1:5:5 feed creates a starter that takes 8–10 hours to peak. It might float at hour 4 because of stiffness, then crash without you noticing the actual peak window.

When the float test gives a false negative

A starter can sink and still be perfectly ready to bake:

  • Stickier than expected. A high-hydration starter (100% water:flour) at peak can be too sticky to detach cleanly from the spoon. It enters the water in a way that traps no air and sinks despite being active.
  • Right after stirring. Stirring deflates the gas. A starter that floated 30 minutes ago might sink right after you mix it. This is normal — the float test should be done with an undisturbed sample.

What the float test actually tells you

The honest read: a floating sample is evidence of recent yeast activity, not proof of peak readiness. A sinking sample is evidence of either pre-activity or post-activity, not proof of failure.

The float test is one signal among several. Treating it as the only test you need is what gets bakers in trouble.

A better readiness checklist

Before you mix dough, look for at least three of the following:

  1. Volume. The starter has at least doubled, ideally tripled, since the last feed. Mark the jar with a rubber band when you feed.
  2. Dome. The surface is rounded upward, not flat or sunken. A peaked starter has a soft dome.
  3. Bubbles on the side and top. Visible activity throughout, not just one side or one layer.
  4. Smell. Pleasant tangy beer-and-yogurt smell. Not vinegar (over-fermented) and not flour-paste (under-fermented).
  5. Clean spoon test. Stir gently. The starter should be elastic and stretchy, like a thick gel — not soupy, not stiff like a paste.
  6. Float test. Optional confirmation if the other signs agree.

If 4 of 6 are positive, your starter is ready. If only 1–2 are positive, it isn't.

Why the wrong-time peak ruins bakes

If your starter peaked 3 hours ago and you're about to mix, you're using a sliding starter — already past the strongest yeast activity, with rising acidity. Bulk will be slower and more sour than expected. The bread will still be bread, but it won't match your schedule.

If your starter hasn't peaked yet — say it's at 70% of peak volume — you're using underdeveloped levain. There's not enough yeast biomass for a strong leavening, and bulk will run long. By the time the dough finally rises, the structure is degraded.

The 1-hour window around peak is when starter is at maximum yeast cell count and minimum bacterial dominance. That's why peak timing matters.

Skip the float test, watch the rise

If you only have time for one observation, watch volume instead of doing the float test. Volume is a continuous measure — you can see the starter at 50%, 100%, 150% rise, and judge progress. Float test is a binary that triggers at an unclear threshold.

A jar with a rubber band marking the start volume teaches you more about your starter than ten float tests. Mark when you feed, mark when you reach 2× volume, mark when it starts to sag back down. You'll learn your starter's peak time within two cycles.

When you can't tell

If after 8 hours a feed shows minimal activity and you can't decide whether to mix or wait, wait. Underactive starter produces underproofed bread, which is worse than the inconvenience of a delayed bake. Feed it again, give it the full window the calculator predicts, and mix when peak is unmistakable. A late bake is recoverable. A weak loaf is not.

The DDT formula: water temperature for perfect dough

Professional bakers do something most home bakers skip: they pre-calculate water temperature so the dough lands at a known temperature after mixing. The math takes ten seconds. The payoff is that bulk fermentation runs on schedule instead of guessing whether the kitchen was warm enough today.

Here's the formula, where it comes from, and how to use it without overcomplicating your bake.

The formula

Water = (Target × 4) − Flour − Room − Friction

Every value is in the same temperature unit (°C is cleaner; °F also works). The "× 4" comes from the four contributors to dough temperature: the water itself, the flour, the room (which heats or cools the dough during mixing), and friction from mixing.

Worked examples

Standard winter day (cold kitchen). Target dough temp 25 °C, flour 18 °C, room 18 °C, hand mixing (3 °C friction).

Water = (25 × 4) − 18 − 18 − 3 = 100 − 39 = 61 °C

That's hot. Run the tap for ten seconds, mix in a splash of cold water until you hit 61 °C. Don't pour boiling water on flour — the gluten won't form properly.

Standard summer day (hot kitchen). Target 25 °C, flour 27 °C, room 27 °C, hand mixing.

Water = 100 − 27 − 27 − 3 = 43 °C

Still warm but not aggressive. Standard tap water might already be in this range.

Stand mixer summer. Same as above but with mixer friction (6 °C).

Water = 100 − 27 − 27 − 6 = 40 °C

The mixer adds heat, so cooler water keeps the dough on target.

Friction values to use

The friction value depends on how much energy goes into mixing:

  • Pinch-and-fold by hand, 2–3 minutes: 1–2 °C
  • Standard hand mix, slap-and-fold for 5 minutes: 3–4 °C
  • Stand mixer, low to medium speed, 8–10 minutes: 5–7 °C
  • Spiral mixer or high-speed dough mixer: 8–12 °C

These are typical values. Measure once after a bake (probe the dough right after mixing, compare to your target) and adjust your friction estimate based on what you actually see.

Why dough temperature matters

Yeast activity scales with temperature. A dough that finishes mixing at 26 °C ferments roughly 25% faster than one at 24 °C, and 50% faster than one at 22 °C. If your recipe's bulk time was written for 25 °C dough and you accidentally landed at 28 °C, the bulk you planned for 6 hours is done in about 4. By the time you check, the dough is overproofed.

The DDT formula prevents this. Land the dough at a known temperature and the schedule from the calculator predicts bulk to within ±20 minutes.

Practical use without overdoing it

You don't have to calculate DDT for every bake. Most home bakers fall into a pattern: tap water plus a known kitchen temperature gives consistent results day after day. The DDT formula matters most when:

  • The season changes. Spring or fall transitions wreck schedules unless you adjust water temperature.
  • You're trying a new recipe. Especially enriched doughs (brioche, panettone, croissant) where temperature precision matters more.
  • Your kitchen is much colder or warmer than usual. A house with under-floor heating or one that gets aggressive sun exposure both throw normal assumptions off.
  • You want competition-quality consistency. Bakery bakers calculate DDT every batch.

Use the calculator's Recipe Lab

The Recipe Lab in the schedule calculator does this math live. Set your target dough temperature, flour temperature, room temperature, and friction estimate. It tells you the water temperature you need. As you change values it updates in real time.

If the calculation tells you to use water below 5 °C or above 60 °C, double-check your inputs. Numbers that extreme usually mean a typo or an unusual kitchen condition worth examining.

A note on flour temperature

Most home bakers assume flour temperature equals room temperature. That's correct for flour stored on a kitchen shelf. It's wrong for flour stored in the fridge or freezer — a habit some bakers adopt to slow rancidity in whole-grain flours.

If you store flour cold, take it out an hour before mixing or measure its actual temperature. Cold flour shifts your DDT calculation by 5–10 °C and you'll undershoot dough temperature significantly.

A note on the water itself

The water you mix into dough doesn't need to be filtered for sourdough — chlorinated tap water mostly works fine. Some bakers swear by leaving water out overnight to dechlorinate; others bake straight from the tap with no problem. Starter health is more sensitive to chlorine than dough is, so if your tap water is heavily chlorinated, dechlorinate the water you feed your starter and use tap water for dough.

The shortcut for cooks who don't like math

If you bake the same recipe every week and your kitchen is consistently in the 21–24 °C range, you don't need DDT. Use lukewarm tap water (35–40 °C) and you'll land in the right ballpark. Keep a notebook with your dough temperature observations and you'll calibrate your tap by feel after 5–6 bakes.

The formula is for the times when you can't trust the shortcut — when the season has shifted, or you're trying a new recipe, or your bread has been off and you don't know why. Plug in the numbers and the temperature variable stops being a mystery.

How to fix dense sourdough (12 causes diagnosed)

Dense sourdough is the most common complaint from home bakers, and it's also one of the most misdiagnosed. Bakers add hydration thinking it will open the crumb. Or they buy a more expensive flour. Or they extend bulk by thirty minutes hoping it helps. Most of those changes don't fix the problem because the problem is rarely what they think it is.

Here's a real diagnostic walkthrough — twelve possible causes ranked by how often they actually explain dense crumb.

1. Underproofed bulk (the cause 70% of the time)

If your loaf comes out dense, this is the first place to look. Bulk fermentation that's too short leaves the dough without enough gas in the gluten structure. The dough enters the oven dense and stays dense.

Symptoms: tight uniform crumb, small even bubbles (or no visible bubbles), dough that felt firm and unyielding when you shaped it.

Fix: extend bulk by 1–2 hours, or raise inoculation by 5%. Track rise visually — aim for 65–75% volume increase from the moment salt was added. The bulk fermentation calculator on this site predicts the right window for your kitchen temperature.

2. Bulk at too low a temperature

Same effect as cause #1, but the diagnostic is different. Maybe your bulk lasted 6 hours like the recipe said, but your kitchen was 18 °C and the recipe was written for 24 °C. At 18 °C, that 6-hour bulk is the equivalent of a 3-hour bulk at 24 °C. Massively underproofed.

Fix: measure dough temperature, not room temperature. A 22 °C kitchen often means 24 °C dough due to mixing friction. Use the actual dough temperature in the calculator and trust the new bulk prediction.

3. Weak starter

A starter that hasn't peaked or is past peak adds too few yeast cells to the dough. Bulk runs slowly, the dough never quite gets there, and you bake an underproofed loaf.

Symptoms: bulk that "should" be done by all timing rules but visually hasn't moved much. Dough feels dense throughout fermentation.

Fix: refresh the starter more often before mixing, use it within 1–2 hours of peak (not 6 hours), and run a 3-feed revival if needed. The Starter tab on this site calculates feed timing backwards from mix time.

4. Wrong flour

Bread flour and all-purpose flour are not interchangeable for sourdough. AP at 10–11% protein cannot hold the gas required for an open crumb. The crumb will be tight and dense regardless of how perfect the rest of your technique is.

Fix: use bread flour with 12–13% protein minimum. King Arthur Bread Flour, Caputo "1," T80 in Europe — anything labelled "bread flour" or "high protein."

5. Hydration too low

A 65% dough is naturally denser than a 75% dough. If you're chasing an open crumb at 65%, you'll never get there. The water isn't there to create the alveolar structure.

Fix: raise hydration to 72–78% for a baseline open-crumb country loaf. Whole grain doughs need 5–8% more.

6. Skipped or weak folds

Folds (stretch-and-fold or coil folds) develop gluten and align the gluten network. Without them, especially at higher hydrations, the dough never builds structure to hold gas.

Fix: 4 sets of folds during the first 2 hours of bulk. Strong folds in sets 1–2, gentle folds in sets 3–4.

7. Pre-shape too tight

A tight pre-shape squeezes gas out of the dough and into a small volume. The gluten network doesn't recover during the brief bench rest, and the loaf never expands properly in the oven.

Fix: pre-shape with a light touch — round the dough loosely, just enough to give it a smooth top.

8. Final shape too tight or too loose

Too tight crushes the gas structure built during bulk. Too loose lets the loaf spread instead of expanding upward, producing a flatter, denser loaf.

Fix: aim for a shape that holds form on the counter for 30 seconds without flattening. The dough should feel taut but not strained.

9. Cold Dutch oven

Sourdough oven spring depends on a hot Dutch oven. A cold Dutch oven (preheated only 20 minutes, or not at all) doesn't deliver the temperature shock that drives expansion. You'll get a dense loaf even when everything else was right.

Fix: preheat the empty Dutch oven for 60 minutes at 250 °C before baking. Set a timer.

10. No steam in the oven

If you bake on an open stone instead of a Dutch oven, the lack of steam lets the crust set early. The loaf can't expand inside the cement-like crust, and you get a dense crumb.

Fix: cover the loaf with an inverted roasting pan for the first 20 minutes, or put a tray of boiling water on the bottom rack. Or switch to a Dutch oven, which traps its own steam.

11. Too cold from retard, then too long out

Some bakers retard their dough then leave it at room temperature for an hour before baking, thinking it needs to "warm up." It doesn't. Cold dough drives more oven spring than warm dough — the temperature differential matters. A retarded dough left out for 60 minutes loses that advantage and bakes denser.

Fix: take the dough straight from the fridge to the hot Dutch oven. Score, transfer, lid on. Done.

12. Underbaked (genuinely uncommon but happens)

Bread that comes out at the visual sign of doneness (deep colour, hollow knock) is sometimes still underbaked. The crumb hasn't fully gelatinised, and what reads as "dense" is actually "doughy."

Fix: probe the loaf at bake-out. Internal temperature should be 96–99 °C. If under, give it 5–10 more minutes lid off.

The diagnostic order

If your bread is dense, work this list top to bottom:

  1. Did the dough rise visibly during bulk? (If no → starter problem)
  2. What was the dough temperature during bulk? (Not the room — the dough)
  3. What flour did you use? (Bread flour minimum)
  4. What was hydration? (72%+ for open crumb)
  5. Did you do 4 folds? (Or did you skip after one?)
  6. How was the pre-shape and final shape tension?
  7. Was the Dutch oven preheated 60 min?
  8. Did you score and bake covered for 20 min?

Most dense loaves are explained by causes 1, 2, or 4. Fix those before chasing exotic explanations.

What to change between bakes

Change one variable at a time. If you raise hydration AND extend bulk AND switch flour all at once, you won't know which fix worked. Pick the most likely cause from the list above, change only that, bake again, observe the result. You'll have a stable open-crumb recipe in 3–4 bakes instead of 15 frustrated attempts.

Starter feeding ratios: 1:1:1 vs 1:2:2 vs 1:5:5

The colon notation describes a feed: starter to flour to water by weight. A 1:2:2 feed at 20 g of starter means 20 g starter + 40 g flour + 40 g water. The ratio you choose changes how long the starter takes to peak and how strong it is when it does. Most home bakers use one ratio for everything, but matching ratio to schedule unlocks more control over your bake.

What changes as the ratio gets bigger

A 1:1:1 feed has a high starter concentration. The yeast and bacteria from the previous feed dominate the substrate. They eat through the available sugars fast and peak quickly.

A 1:5:5 feed has a low starter concentration. The same population now has 5 times more food to work through. They peak slowly, but the population grows substantially before they run out. The peak is bigger, the starter is stronger, and the time to peak is much longer.

In between, 1:2:2 sits at the sweet spot for most home bakers — peaks in a manageable window, produces enough starter to bake with, and gives enough food that the starter doesn't crash within hours.

Time to peak at typical kitchen temperatures

These are approximate, calibrated for a healthy starter at 22 °C kitchen temperature. Cooler kitchens add time; warmer kitchens cut it.

Ratio Peak time at 22 °C Use case
1:1:1 3–4 h Refresh feed before a build feed; quick maintenance
1:2:2 5–6 h Standard pre-bake feed; the home baker default
1:3:3 7–8 h Overnight peak (feed in evening, bake midday)
1:5:5 8–10 h Long overnight; or for low-acid starter
1:10:10 12–16 h Very weak starter food; less common

Halve the kitchen temperature relative to the rule and roughly double these times. At 16 °C, a 1:2:2 takes 10–12 hours instead of 5–6.

When to use a 1:1:1

This is a tight feed. Use it when:

  • You need to bake same-day and don't have time to wait 8 hours. A 1:1:1 in the morning peaks by lunch.
  • The starter has been weak. Concentration feeds (1:1:1 or even 1:0.5:0.5) help rebuild yeast population from a sluggish starter.
  • As a refresh feed before a build feed. When you have a starter that's been in the fridge, a 1:1:1 wakes it up before a 1:2:2 builds the volume you need.

The downside: 1:1:1 starters peak fast and crash fast. If you miss the window by an hour, you're using sliding starter, which produces underproofed dough.

When to use a 1:2:2

The default. Use it when:

  • You feed the night before for a morning bake — peaks 5–6 hours after feeding, sits at near-peak for another hour or two.
  • You feed in the morning for an evening bake — same window.
  • Your kitchen is at typical room temperature (20–24 °C).
  • You don't have a strong reason to use a different ratio.

Most published sourdough recipes assume a 1:2:2 feed unless they say otherwise.

When to use a 1:5:5 or higher

A loose feed. Use it when:

  • You want a long peak window — 8–10 hours. Useful for overnight feeds where you want peak to coincide with a morning mix.
  • The starter has been over-acidic. A 1:5:5 dilutes accumulated acids and produces a milder, sweeter starter.
  • You want maximum yeast strength. Bigger food supply means more yeast cells produced before crash.
  • You're storing the starter at room temp between bakes — bigger feeds last longer between feeds.

The downside: longer wait times. If you misjudge timing, you can be waiting hours for peak.

Why peak timing beats average timing

The 1-hour window centred on peak is when starter is at maximum yeast cell count and minimum bacterial dominance. A starter used 2 hours past peak ferments the dough more slowly (yeast cells starting to die back) and produces a more acidic loaf. A starter used 2 hours before peak doesn't have enough yeast biomass yet — bulk will run long.

Choose the ratio that puts peak at the time you want to mix. The Starter tab on this site does this math: tell it when you need peak, your kitchen temperature, and your preferred feed count, and it gives you the exact times and amounts.

Multi-feed building

For a starter coming out of the fridge after a week or more, one feed isn't enough. The yeast population is depressed and a single feed produces a peak too weak for reliable baking.

The standard 2-feed protocol:

  1. Refresh feed (1:1:1 or 1:0.5:0.5): small ratio, concentrates the surviving population. Peak in 4–6 hours.
  2. Build feed (1:2:2): at the moment refresh peaks, build up to the volume you need for mixing. Peak in 5–6 hours.

Total: 9–12 hours from fridge to bakeable starter. The Starter tab schedules this automatically.

For very weak or fridge-forgotten starters (>3 weeks), use a 3-feed protocol:

  1. Wake feed (1:1:1, small): wake up dormant population. 8–12 hours.
  2. Refresh feed (1:1:1 or 1:2:2): build population. 5–6 hours.
  3. Build feed (1:2:2): target volume. 5–6 hours.

Total: 18–24 hours. Patient, but reliable.

Quick rule of thumb

If you want to remember just one thing: 1:2:2 covers 90% of bakes. Use it as your default. Only switch to 1:1:1 when you're in a hurry, or 1:5:5 when you want a slow, big peak.

A starter you understand at one ratio is more useful than a starter you've never timed correctly across three.

Stretch and fold: how many, when, and why

The stretch-and-fold technique replaces kneading in sourdough. You don't develop the gluten by working the dough on a counter for 10 minutes; you develop it by gently lifting and folding the dough over itself a few times during the first half of bulk fermentation. Done right, it's the easiest dough-handling technique in baking. Done wrong (or skipped), it's the reason for half the dense and flat sourdough loaves home bakers post about.

Here's what's actually happening, how many folds you need, and when to stop.

What folding does

Mixing combines flour and water and starts forming gluten — long chains of protein molecules that trap fermentation gas. But the gluten formed during mixing alone is disorganised. The strands are tangled, weak, and uneven.

Folding aligns the gluten. Each fold stretches the dough, lengthening the protein chains and getting them parallel. Repeated folds layer aligned gluten on aligned gluten. After 4 sets, the dough has organized structure capable of holding its shape, expanding upward instead of outward, and trapping gas in a way that produces an open crumb.

You're not adding strength by kneading. You're letting the dough develop strength on its own and just helping it organise.

How to do a stretch-and-fold

Wet hands lightly. Reach under one side of the dough, lift it up until it stretches as far as it'll go without tearing, fold it over the centre. Rotate the bowl 90 degrees. Repeat — three more times for a complete set of four folds. Cover the bowl, walk away.

That's one "set." Most schedules call for 4 sets total during the first 2 hours of bulk fermentation.

When to do them

The standard schedule, with timing measured from when you mixed in the salt and started bulk:

  • Set 1: 30 minutes in. The dough is sticky, the folds feel rough. That's normal.
  • Set 2: 60 minutes in. Noticeably smoother. Gluten is starting to organise.
  • Set 3: 90 minutes in. Smooth, supple, holds shape between folds.
  • Set 4: 120 minutes in. Should feel taut, almost springy. The dough holds the folded shape for a full minute before relaxing.

After set 4, leave the dough alone for the rest of bulk. Folding past this point doesn't help and can actually break down the gas structure you're trying to preserve.

How many folds is "right"

Most home recipes call for 4 sets. This is the workable default. But the right answer depends on hydration and flour:

  • 65–72% hydration, bread flour: 3 sets is enough. Strong flour develops fast.
  • 72–80%, bread flour: 4 sets, the classic schedule.
  • 80–85%, strong bread flour: 5–6 sets, sometimes including coil folds.
  • 85%+, high-hydration: 6+ sets of coil folds, every 30 minutes.

The signal isn't a number — it's the dough itself. You stop folding when the dough is smooth, glossy, holds its shape when poked, and resists being deformed. If a dough still feels slack after 4 sets, do a 5th. If it felt strong after 2 sets, you can stop early.

Stretch-and-fold vs coil fold

The basic stretch-and-fold described above works for doughs up to about 75% hydration. Above that, the dough is wet enough that lifting it from underneath drags through the dough and creates a mess.

For wetter doughs, switch to coil folds: with wet hands, lift the dough from the centre upward, letting the bottom drape down. Then lift again from a slightly different angle. The dough essentially folds under itself — same result, less pulling.

Most recipes for very high-hydration loaves (85%+ ciabatta, pan de cristal) specify coil folds explicitly.

What if you skip folds entirely

Skipping folds in a 75% hydration country loaf produces a loaf that's flat, dense, and structurally weak. You can taste the bread tested it but the crumb won't be open and the shape won't hold. The fermentation works fine — but without organised gluten, the gas escapes laterally instead of expanding the crumb upward.

For very low-hydration doughs (under 65%) you can sometimes get away with no folds because the dough is so stiff it has structure on its own. For sourdough, fold.

Folding and bulk timing

Folding doesn't slow or speed bulk fermentation directly. It changes the structural quality of the bread, not the chemistry of fermentation. So when the calculator predicts 6 hours of bulk at 22 °C, that 6 hours is correct whether you fold 3 times or 5 times.

What folds DO change is when bulk visually appears done. A well-folded dough holds gas better and shows visible rise sooner — you might see the 50% rise mark slightly earlier than expected. A poorly folded dough loses gas as fast as it makes it and may never hit 50% rise visibly even at proper proof time.

Common mistakes

Folding too late. All 4 sets should be done by hour 2 of bulk. Folding at hour 3 or 4 disrupts the gas already accumulating in the structure.

Folding too aggressively. Stretch the dough until it's about to tear, not past. Tearing the dough breaks the gluten you're trying to develop.

Skipping the 90-minute set because the dough already looks good. It's tempting, but the discipline of all 4 sets produces noticeably stronger dough than 3.

Using a dry counter for high-hydration coil folds. The dough sticks. Use wet hands, do all folds in the bowl.

Folding after bulk has ended. Once the dough is at 65–75% rise, leave it alone. Pre-shape and bench rest are next, not more folds.

Use the calculator's schedule

The schedule generated by the calculator on this site lists each fold with its specific time. Follow that schedule and you don't have to remember anything — fold 1 at 21:10, fold 2 at 21:40, etc. After bulk start, your job is just to fold on schedule and watch for the rise.

The discipline of structured folds is what separates good sourdough from disappointing sourdough. It's the technique most worth getting right because it costs almost no extra time and changes the bread fundamentally.

Scoring sourdough: ear, bloom, and the basic patterns

A scored loaf opens where you tell it to. An unscored loaf tears wherever the dough is weakest — usually a ragged blowout along the side, sometimes a flat top with no rise at all. Sourdough scoring is how you control that explosive expansion in the first 10 minutes of baking, when steam and trapped CO₂ are pushing the crumb upward against a crust that's rapidly setting.

Done well, you get an ear (a raised lip of crust along the cut) and bloom (the dramatic opening and lift around the score). Done poorly, the cut closes back up, the loaf goes flat, or the crust splits sideways. Most fixes are mechanical, not mystical.

The tools

A real lame with a fresh double-edged razor blade. Not a paring knife, not a serrated bread knife, not a butter knife. The blade needs to glide through wet dough without dragging. A dull blade snags, deflates the surface, and gives you a closed-up score every time.

Swap blades every 5–10 loaves. They cost pennies. A blade that scored beautifully last week may be dull enough today to ruin a bake.

What temperature dough scores best

Cold. After a 12–16 hour cold retard at 38°F (3°C), the surface is firm and tacky but not sticky. The blade slices cleanly instead of dragging through soft, gassy dough.

If you're baking same-day without a cold retard, chill the shaped loaf in the freezer for 20–30 minutes before scoring. The skin firms up enough to take a clean cut.

Angle and depth

Two cuts, two purposes:

  • Ear cut: blade held at roughly 20–30° from the surface, almost parallel to the loaf. This creates a flap of dough that lifts during oven spring — that's your ear. Depth: about 1/4 inch (6 mm).
  • Decorative cut: blade held at 90°, straight down. Used for wheat stalks, leaves, and patterns where you want clean lines, not dramatic lift. Depth: 1/8 inch (3 mm), shallower.

Most beginners cut too deep and too vertical. A vertical cut 1/2 inch deep just opens up and stays open — no ear, just a canyon. A shallow angled cut produces the lift.

The basic patterns

Single slash (the workhorse)

One off-center cut running 3/4 of the length of the loaf, at a 30° angle. This is the classic batard score. It directs all the oven spring into one ear and gives you maximum bloom in one direction. If you bake one loaf shape, learn this one first.

Cross or square

Two perpendicular cuts on a boule. Vertical, not angled. Produces four "petals" that open evenly. Forgiving — works even if your shaping is mediocre because expansion is distributed.

Wheat stalk

A central spine cut down the loaf, then short angled cuts branching off either side. All shallow (about 1/8 inch), all at 90°. Decorative, not structural — pair it with a single deep ear cut along one side if you want bloom too.

Coil or spiral on a boule

One continuous curving cut from center outward. Looks impressive, scores forgivingly. Keep it shallow.

Why your loaves aren't blooming

Run through this list before blaming the score:

  1. Underproofed: dough has nowhere to expand from internally — score opens but the crumb stays dense. Look for a crumb that bloomed but with a tight, gummy interior.
  2. Overproofed: no spring left. The score will sit flat or even spread sideways. Crumb will be open but flat overall.
  3. Weak gluten / poor shaping: surface tension is what holds the loaf upright while it springs. A slack, sloppy shape blows out the bottom.
  4. Not enough steam: without steam in the first 15 minutes, the crust sets before bloom can happen. A Dutch oven solves this; an open-bake without a steam source rarely produces a strong ear.
  5. Oven not hot enough: 475–500°F (245–260°C) for the covered phase. A 425°F oven won't drive enough spring.

If you're not sure whether your dough is at the right hydration to hold a clean score, the hydration calculator on this page can help you dial in a recipe — wetter doughs (78%+) need firmer chilling and a sharper blade; drier doughs (68–72%) score easily but spring less dramatically.

What to do today

  1. Buy a pack of double-edged razor blades. Replace yours.
  2. Cold-retard your next loaf overnight, score it straight from the fridge.
  3. Make one off-center slash, blade at 30°, 1/4 inch deep, in one confident motion.
  4. Bake covered at 500°F for 20 minutes, then uncovered at 450°F until done.

One variable at a time. If the ear shows up, you've got your baseline — start playing with patterns from there.

Autolyse vs fermentolyse: the math behind both

You've probably read that you should autolyse your dough for 30 minutes, an hour, maybe overnight. Then someone on Instagram tells you autolyse is pointless and fermentolyse is better. Both camps are partially right, and the difference comes down to math: how much water hits the flour, when the starter shows up, and how long enzymes have to work before fermentation acidifies the dough.

What each one actually is

Autolyse is flour and water only. No starter, no salt. You mix until there's no dry flour, then rest. Raymond Calvel coined the term in 1974 to fix the over-oxidized, flavorless baguettes that resulted from high-speed industrial mixing. The rest period lets the flour fully hydrate and gives enzymes (amylase, protease) time to work before any mixing develops the gluten further.

Fermentolyse adds the starter at the same time as the flour and water. Salt is still held back. You rest, then add salt later. It's a shortcut: you get hydration and some enzymatic activity, but fermentation starts immediately.

The "salt later" rule for both exists because salt tightens gluten and slows protease activity. You want the flour soft and extensible during the rest.

The math: enzymes vs acid

Sourdough flour contains amylase, which breaks starch into sugars, and protease, which breaks proteins into smaller chains, increasing extensibility. Both enzymes work best around pH 5.5–6.5 — basically the pH of plain flour-water dough.

Once you add starter, fermentation drops the pH:

  • Starter at 100% hydration, 20% inoculation: dough pH drops from ~6.0 to ~5.0 in roughly 2–3 hours at 76°F.
  • At pH below 5.0, protease activity slows. Below 4.5, it nearly stops.

That's the core tradeoff. Pure autolyse keeps the dough in the enzyme sweet spot longer. Fermentolyse trades some enzymatic time for an earlier start on fermentation.

How long does each actually need?

For autolyse, the useful range:

  • 20–30 minutes: full flour hydration. Gluten is more extensible. This is the minimum return on investment.
  • 1–2 hours: noticeable protease activity. Dough feels slacker, smoother.
  • 4+ hours: protease has done significant work. Dough may feel sticky or weak, especially with weaker flours (10–11% protein).
  • Overnight (8–12 hours, refrigerated): only with strong flour (12.5%+ protein) and cold temperatures to suppress wild yeast and bacteria already on the flour. Otherwise the dough sours and weakens.

For fermentolyse, the window is shorter because the clock is the starter:

  • 30–60 minutes is the standard. Long enough to hydrate flour and let enzymes work briefly, short enough that fermentation hasn't acidified the dough.
  • Beyond 90 minutes at room temperature with a 15–20% inoculation, you're effectively just doing bulk fermentation with no salt — which weakens gluten via prolonged protease activity in fermenting dough.

When to pick which

Use autolyse when:

  • Your flour is high-protein (12.5%+) and you want more extensibility
  • You're making baguettes, ciabatta, or open-crumb hearth breads where extensibility matters
  • You want to separate hydration time from fermentation time for scheduling control
  • You're doing high hydration (78%+) and need the flour to absorb water fully before adding anything else

Use fermentolyse when:

  • Your flour is weaker (10–11.5% protein) and a long autolyse would degrade it
  • You're using a lot of whole grain, where enzymes are already abundant and you don't need extra protease time
  • You want a more streamlined timeline — one rest, then bulk
  • You bake at warmer temperatures (78°F+) where dough acidifies fast

A worked example

Say you're mixing 1000g flour, 78% hydration, 20% starter, 2% salt, at 76°F.

Autolyse path:

  • Mix 1000g flour + 780g water. Rest 1 hour.
  • Add 200g starter and 20g salt with 20g reserved water. Mix.
  • Bulk: ~4.5 hours.

Fermentolyse path:

  • Mix 1000g flour + 780g water + 200g starter. Rest 45 minutes.
  • Add 20g salt with 20g reserved water. Mix.
  • Bulk: ~4 hours (the 45 min counts as part of fermentation).

Total time to shape is similar. Crumb structure can differ slightly — autolyse loaves often show a hair more openness; fermentolyse loaves can have marginally more flavor depth from the longer cumulative ferment.

If you want to plug your own numbers in — flour weight, hydration, inoculation — the calculator on this page handles the conversions and shows you when in your timeline each step lands.

What to do today

  1. Pick one method and run it for three bakes back to back. Don't switch variables.
  2. Note flour protein percentage on the bag. If it's under 12%, cap your autolyse at 45 minutes.
  3. Check dough pH if you have a meter. Aim for 4.1–4.3 at the end of bulk.
  4. If your crumb is tight, try a longer autolyse (90 min) before changing hydration.
  5. If your dough goes slack and sticky during bulk, shorten the autolyse or switch to fermentolyse.

Pick the method that fits your flour and your schedule. The math just tells you the limits.

Whole wheat sourdough: hydration, timing, and protein

Whole wheat behaves nothing like white flour. The bran absorbs more water, cuts gluten strands as it hydrates, and ferments roughly 30% faster because of the enzymes and wild yeast living on the wheat kernel. If you're getting dense, gummy loaves with weak oven spring, the fix usually isn't more skill — it's adjusting hydration, timing, and your expectations about protein.

This article covers what actually matters: how much water to add, when to shape, and why a 14% protein whole wheat flour can produce a worse loaf than an 11% one.

Why whole wheat is harder than white

When you mill the whole wheat berry, you get three things: endosperm (the starchy interior that becomes white flour), germ (the embryo, full of oils and enzymes), and bran (the fibrous outer layers). The bran is the problem child.

Bran particles are sharp. As you mix and stretch the dough, they slice through gluten sheets like little razors. This is why whole wheat doughs feel strong at first, then go slack, then never quite recover the elasticity you'd expect from the protein content on the label.

The germ adds enzyme activity — particularly amylase and protease — that speeds fermentation and weakens gluten over time. Whole wheat ferments faster and the gluten gets weaker the longer it sits. Both clocks are ticking against you.

Hydration: the numbers that actually work

A standard white sourdough at 75% hydration will absorb similar water in a whole wheat blend, but the dough will feel very different over time. Bran keeps drinking water for hours after mixing. A dough that feels stiff at mixing can feel slack at shaping.

Here are working hydration ranges by whole wheat percentage:

Whole wheat % Target hydration Notes
20–30% 75–78% Behaves close to white; standard timing works
40–50% 78–82% Sweet spot for most home bakers
60–75% 82–87% Reduce bulk time by 15–20%
100% 85–95% Hardest to handle; expect a tighter crumb

These are starting points, not gospel. Stone-ground flour absorbs more than commercial roller-milled flour. Freshly milled flour absorbs less the first few days (the bran hasn't fully hydrated through storage) and more after a week of rest. If you mill your own, give the flour 24–48 hours before baking with it.

Autolyse, or fermentolyse?

For whole wheat, skip the long autolyse. A 30–60 minute autolyse with white flour builds gluten beautifully. With whole wheat, the bran enzymes start breaking down protein during a long rest, leaving you with a slacker, stickier dough.

Two better options:

  1. Short autolyse (20–30 minutes) with just flour and water, then add salt and levain.
  2. Fermentolyse — mix everything (flour, water, levain, salt) at once and start bulk immediately.

The fermentolyse approach gives you more control because fermentation acidifies the dough, which actually strengthens gluten against the bran's enzymatic assault.

Timing: shorter bulk, longer cold proof

The single biggest mistake with whole wheat is treating the bulk fermentation timeline like white sourdough. A bulk that takes 5 hours with white flour at 76°F might be done in 3.5 hours with a 50% whole wheat dough at the same temperature.

Watch the dough, not the clock. You're looking for:

  • 20–40% rise (not the 50–75% you'd push white dough to)
  • Visible bubbles on the surface and sides of a clear container
  • A jiggly, domed top when you tap the bulk container

If you wait for the same volume increase you get with white flour, you're already past peak. Whole wheat doesn't recover from over-fermentation — the gluten is too compromised by the bran. Underproofing slightly is far more forgiving than overproofing.

The cold retard is your friend

A 12–16 hour cold retard at 38–40°F does more for whole wheat sourdough than for any other bread. Cold slows the enzymes, lets flavors develop, and firms the dough up enough that you can actually score it without it deflating.

If you have any doubt about bulk timing, err on the early side and let the cold proof finish the job. A loaf shaped slightly underproofed and retarded 14 hours will outperform a perfectly bulk-proofed loaf retarded 6 hours nine times out of ten.

Protein: the label lies (sort of)

Whole wheat flour labeled at 14% protein doesn't behave like white bread flour at 14% protein. Two reasons:

  1. Not all that protein is gluten-forming. The bran and germ contain non-gluten proteins that show up on a Kjeldahl nitrogen test but contribute nothing to dough structure.
  2. Variety matters more than percentage. Hard red winter wheat at 12.5% protein often produces stronger dough than hard red spring at 14%, because of the gluten quality, not quantity.

For sourdough, look for:

  • Hard red winter or hard red spring wheat for structure
  • Hard white wheat for milder flavor with similar strength
  • "Bread flour" or "high-protein" whole wheat rather than all-purpose whole wheat

Avoid soft white wheat (sometimes called pastry wheat) for sourdough — it has weak, extensible gluten suited to muffins and cookies, not bread.

Fresh-milled vs. commercial

Fresh-milled whole wheat has more enzyme activity, more flavor, and weaker gluten than commercial whole wheat that's been sitting for months. The germ oils oxidize and stabilize over time, which dulls flavor but improves baking performance.

If you mill your own:

  • Sift out 10–15% of the coarsest bran for better volume
  • Use slightly less water at first; the flour absorbs more over time
  • Reduce bulk fermentation by another 10–15%

A working formula for 50% whole wheat

This is the formula I recommend to bakers transitioning from white sourdough. Use the calculator on this page to scale it to your loaf size.

  • 250g whole wheat flour (50%)
  • 250g bread flour (50%)
  • 400g water (80%)
  • 100g levain at 100% hydration (20%)
  • 10g salt (2%)

Method:

  1. Mix everything except salt. Rest 30 minutes.
  2. Add salt with 15g additional water. Mix until incorporated.
  3. Bulk ferment at 76–78°F for 3.5–4.5 hours.
  4. Stretch and fold 3 times in the first 90 minutes, then leave alone.
  5. Shape when dough is jiggly and risen 30%.
  6. Cold retard 12–16 hours at 38°F.
  7. Bake at 500°F covered for 20 minutes, then 450°F uncovered for 22–25 minutes.

Once this works consistently, push the whole wheat to 70%, then 100%. Don't jump.

Common problems and what they mean

Gummy crumb

Almost always underbaked or underproofed. Whole wheat needs to hit 207–210°F internal temperature, not the 200°F that's fine for white sourdough. The extra bran holds moisture and needs more heat to fully set.

Dense loaf with no oven spring

Usually overproofed bulk, occasionally weak starter. If your starter doubles in 4+ hours at 78°F, it's not strong enough for whole wheat. Feed twice daily for 3–4 days before baking.

Sticky, unworkable dough at shaping

Either too much water for your specific flour, too long an autolyse, or overproofed bulk. Try the same formula at 5% less hydration next time and use a 20-minute fermentolyse instead.

Pale crust

Whole wheat browns differently than white. The sugars from amylase activity get consumed during the longer effective fermentation. Push your bake temperature higher (500°F start) and consider a longer cold retard, which preserves more residual sugars for crust color.

Common questions

Can I use whole wheat starter for white bread, and vice versa?

Yes. The flour you feed your starter affects flavor but not function. A whole wheat starter will ferment slightly faster and add a nuttier taste to any dough. Many bakers keep a single whole wheat starter because it's more active and resilient than a white one.

Should I use a 100% whole wheat starter for whole wheat bread?

It helps but isn't essential. A more active starter handles the faster fermentation of whole wheat dough better. If your starter is sluggish, switch to whole wheat or rye feedings for a week before baking a whole wheat loaf.

Why does my 100% whole wheat loaf have a tight crumb no matter what?

That's largely the nature of the flour. Bran physically prevents the large, open air pockets you get in white sourdough. You can improve it with higher hydration (88%+) and gentle handling, but a pillowy ear-and-honeycomb crumb isn't realistic at 100% whole wheat. Aim for even, medium-fine crumb instead.

How much can I push hydration before the dough becomes unworkable?

With 50% whole wheat, you can go to 85% comfortably. With 100%, 90% is the practical ceiling for most home bakers. Beyond that you need a stiff levain, lamination instead of stretch-and-folds, and a banneton dusted heavily with rice flour. Diminishing returns set in fast.

Is sifted whole wheat (high-extraction) easier to work with?

Much easier. Sifting out 10–20% of the coarse bran gives you most of the flavor and nutrition of whole wheat with significantly better gluten development. T85 or high-extraction flours (around 85% extraction rate) are the sweet spot for bakers who want whole wheat character without the handling difficulty.

Do I need vital wheat gluten?

No. It's a workaround that produces a slightly rubbery crumb. Better starter, better timing, and a hard red wheat variety will do more than added gluten ever does.

What to do this week

Start with one change, not five. If you've been baking white sourdough and want to move toward whole wheat:

  1. Bake your usual recipe with 30% whole wheat substitution at +3% hydration. Note how it differs.
  2. Shorten your bulk by 30 minutes next bake, regardless of how the dough looks.
  3. Push your internal temperature to 208°F instead of pulling at 200°F.

After three bakes at 30%, move to 50%. Track timing and hydration in a notebook — whole wheat varies enough between brands and seasons that you can't memorize a single formula. You're building a feel for how this flour, in your kitchen, at this temperature, wants to behave.

That feel is the whole game.

Common questions.

How accurate is this schedule?

Treat it as a planning estimate, not a stopwatch. Real fermentation depends on starter strength, flour protein content, dough actual temperature (often warmer than ambient), and humidity. Use the schedule to plan your day, then judge bulk by visual cues: 50–75% rise, jiggle, visible dome.

Why is bulk longer at lower temperatures?

Yeast and bacterial enzymes work faster as temperature rises. The calculator uses a Q10 of roughly 2.2, meaning fermentation rate doubles every 8°C. At 16°C bulk takes roughly twice as long as at 24°C with everything else equal.

What does "active and peaked" starter mean?

A peaked starter has at least doubled, shows a domed surface with visible bubbles, and floats in water. Use within an hour or two of peak. A recently fed starter still needs four to six hours to ripen. Cold starter from the fridge needs to warm up and feed before it's usable.

Can I skip the cold retard?

Yes. Set retard to zero and the calculator plans a same-day bake with final proof at room temperature, typically 1–2 hours after final shaping. You'll lose some flavor complexity but gain flexibility on baking day.

What's the difference between feed counts?

One feed works when your starter is already active or recently fed. Two feeds is the standard for a starter taken from the fridge — a refresh feed wakes it up, then a build feed produces the volume you need. Three feeds is for starters that have been stored more than a week and need extra revival cycles.

What hydration should I start with?

For your first ten loaves stay around 70–75% with bread flour. Higher hydration (80%+) demands stronger flour and better technique. Whole wheat absorbs more — bump hydration 5–8% if you're adding 20%+ whole grain. The Recipe Lab updates grams live so you can see the shift.
