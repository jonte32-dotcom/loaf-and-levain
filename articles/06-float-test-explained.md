# The float test: when is your starter actually ready?

Drop a teaspoon of starter into a glass of water. If it floats, your starter is ready to bake. If it sinks, wait. That's the float test in one sentence — and it's the most-cited starter readiness test online. It's also more nuanced than it looks. Here's when it works, when it lies, and what to do when you can't trust it.

## Why the float test works (most of the time)

A starter at peak is full of CO2 trapped in a gluten-protein network. The bubbles make it less dense than water, so a small piece floats. When the starter has crashed past peak, it's lost most of its gas — the bubbles burst and the dough sags. Now it's denser than water, and it sinks.

So a positive float test usually means: "this starter has enough yeast activity to leaven bread." A negative test usually means: "this starter is either pre-peak (not ready yet) or post-peak (already crashed)."

Note the word "usually." The test has failure modes.

## When the float test gives a false positive

A starter can float and still be a poor candidate for baking:

- **Cold starter.** A starter pulled straight from the fridge often floats — the cold thickens it and CO2 from previous activity is still trapped. This doesn't mean it's strong enough to leaven your dough.
- **Whole-rye starter.** Rye creates such viscous, gummy starter that even crashed rye can float. Don't use float test on rye-only starters.
- **Very stiff starter.** A 50% hydration starter (more flour than water) is dense and gas-trapping. It can float for hours after crashing.
- **Recent feed at high ratio.** A 1:5:5 feed creates a starter that takes 8–10 hours to peak. It might float at hour 4 because of stiffness, then crash without you noticing the actual peak window.

## When the float test gives a false negative

A starter can sink and still be perfectly ready to bake:

- **Stickier than expected.** A high-hydration starter (100% water:flour) at peak can be too sticky to detach cleanly from the spoon. It enters the water in a way that traps no air and sinks despite being active.
- **Right after stirring.** Stirring deflates the gas. A starter that floated 30 minutes ago might sink right after you mix it. This is normal — the float test should be done with an undisturbed sample.

## What the float test actually tells you

The honest read: a floating sample is *evidence* of recent yeast activity, not proof of peak readiness. A sinking sample is *evidence* of either pre-activity or post-activity, not proof of failure.

The float test is one signal among several. Treating it as the only test you need is what gets bakers in trouble.

## A better readiness checklist

Before you mix dough, look for at least three of the following:

1. **Volume.** The starter has at least doubled, ideally tripled, since the last feed. Mark the jar with a rubber band when you feed.
2. **Dome.** The surface is rounded upward, not flat or sunken. A peaked starter has a soft dome.
3. **Bubbles on the side and top.** Visible activity throughout, not just one side or one layer.
4. **Smell.** Pleasant tangy beer-and-yogurt smell. Not vinegar (over-fermented) and not flour-paste (under-fermented).
5. **Clean spoon test.** Stir gently. The starter should be elastic and stretchy, like a thick gel — not soupy, not stiff like a paste.
6. **Float test.** Optional confirmation if the other signs agree.

If 4 of 6 are positive, your starter is ready. If only 1–2 are positive, it isn't.

## Why the wrong-time peak ruins bakes

If your starter peaked 3 hours ago and you're about to mix, you're using a sliding starter — already past the strongest yeast activity, with rising acidity. Bulk will be slower and more sour than expected. The bread will still be bread, but it won't match your schedule.

If your starter hasn't peaked yet — say it's at 70% of peak volume — you're using underdeveloped levain. There's not enough yeast biomass for a strong leavening, and bulk will run long. By the time the dough finally rises, the structure is degraded.

The 1-hour window around peak is when starter is at maximum yeast cell count and minimum bacterial dominance. That's why peak timing matters.

## Skip the float test, watch the rise

If you only have time for one observation, watch volume instead of doing the float test. Volume is a continuous measure — you can see the starter at 50%, 100%, 150% rise, and judge progress. Float test is a binary that triggers at an unclear threshold.

A jar with a rubber band marking the start volume teaches you more about your starter than ten float tests. Mark when you feed, mark when you reach 2× volume, mark when it starts to sag back down. You'll learn your starter's peak time within two cycles.

## When you can't tell

If after 8 hours a feed shows minimal activity and you can't decide whether to mix or wait, *wait*. Underactive starter produces underproofed bread, which is worse than the inconvenience of a delayed bake. Feed it again, give it the full window the calculator predicts, and mix when peak is unmistakable. A late bake is recoverable. A weak loaf is not.
