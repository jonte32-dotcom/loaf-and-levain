# How to revive a forgotten sourdough starter

You opened the fridge and there it is — your starter. Last fed three weeks ago. Maybe two months ago. The hooch is dark, there's a sour smell, possibly a thin grey skin on top. The good news: unless there's actual mould (fuzzy, coloured spots), it's recoverable. Wild yeast and lactic bacteria are surprisingly hard to kill. They go dormant, not dead.

Here's how to bring it back, and how to know when it's ready to bake with again.

## First, decide if it's dead or just sleeping

**Probably alive (recoverable):**
- Dark liquid (hooch) on top — that's just alcohol from fermentation
- Strong vinegar or acetone smell
- Grey or brownish skin on top
- No bubbles visible

**Probably dead or contaminated (start over):**
- Pink, orange, green, or fuzzy growth — that's mould
- Bright pink streaks — that's bacterial contamination
- Smell of rotting garbage or sweaty socks (not just sour or alcoholic)

If you see any of the second list, throw it out and start fresh. A new starter is 7 days of effort and there's no point fighting contamination.

## The 3-feed revival protocol

Most fridge-forgotten starters need three feeds at room temperature to come fully back. Here's the sequence:

### Feed 1: the wake-up

Pour off the hooch. Scrape out a small amount — about 10 g — of the surviving starter from underneath the surface. Discard the rest. The reason: you don't want to dilute weak yeast with a huge amount of fresh flour and water; you want to concentrate what's alive.

Mix that 10 g with 10 g flour and 10 g water (a 1:1:1 ratio). Cover loosely. Leave it at room temperature for **8–12 hours**.

You probably won't see much. Maybe a few bubbles, maybe nothing. That's normal. The yeast is rebuilding population.

### Feed 2: the build

After 8–12 hours, your wake-up feed should at least show some bubbles. Take 10 g of it, add 20 g flour and 20 g water (1:2:2 ratio). Cover. Leave at room temperature.

This feed should show clear life within **6–8 hours** at 22°C — visible doming, bubbles on the side, a clean fermented smell instead of sharp vinegar.

If nothing happens after 12 hours, repeat Feed 2. Some starters need an extra cycle.

### Feed 3: the final build

When Feed 2 has clearly doubled and is at peak, do one more feed at the ratio you'll bake with. For most home bakers that's 1:2:2 again. Take 20 g, add 40 g flour and 40 g water. Wait for peak — usually **5–6 hours** at 22°C.

A peaked starter looks like this: domed top, full of bubbles, smells like beer and yogurt, and floats in water (the float test). This is the starter you bake with.

## Common revival problems and what they mean

**Acetone smell, no rise.** The starter is starving. It's eaten all the available sugars and produced alcohol as a stress response. Discard most of it and feed at 1:5:5 (a strong feed) — give it lots of food.

**Bubbles but no rise.** Yeast is alive but population is low. Feed at 1:1:1 (a tight feed) for two cycles to concentrate it, then build up.

**Big rise, then total collapse and watery surface.** Starter is over-fermented — it peaked and crashed before you got to it. Keep feeding at the same ratio but catch it earlier next cycle. The starter isn't broken; you're just timing the next feed wrong.

**Smells like nail polish.** Acetone again. Same fix as above — feed it more, more often.

**Starter peaks, then doesn't rise next feed.** This usually means the previous batch wasn't actually mature, or the room temperature changed. Don't panic. Feed it at the same ratio, give it more time, and re-check.

## The maintenance schedule that prevents this

Once your starter is healthy again, you have two options to avoid forgetting it:

**Option A: bake weekly.** Feed Friday evening, mix dough Saturday afternoon, bake Sunday morning. Leftover starter goes back in the fridge for a week. This is the simplest schedule and keeps a starter happy.

**Option B: bake monthly.** Refresh the starter once a week — pull from fridge, do one feed at room temp until peaked, put back. This adds 10 minutes a week and keeps the starter ready for an unplanned bake.

The trap is keeping a starter "for emergencies" without ever baking. Yeast needs to eat. A starter fed once every 6 weeks isn't being maintained — it's slowly dying. If you don't bake regularly, give the starter to a friend who does, and start a fresh one when you're ready to commit again.

## When to give up and start over

After three full revival cycles (three rounds of all three feeds), if the starter still won't peak reliably or doubles within 8 hours at 22°C, it's time to start fresh. Whole-rye flour and water for 5–7 days will give you a working starter. The fork in the road is rarely worth more than 24 hours of revival effort.
