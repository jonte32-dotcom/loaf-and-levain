# Winter sourdough: surviving a cold kitchen

Your starter doubles in 4 hours in July. In January, it barely budges overnight. Same flour, same feeding ratio, same jar — but a 16°C kitchen ferments at roughly one-third the speed of a 24°C kitchen. Cold kitchen sourdough isn't broken sourdough. It's just slower, and the fix is mostly about understanding how temperature drives fermentation, then nudging the dough toward conditions it actually wants.

This guide covers what changes below 20°C, how to find or create warm spots, how to adjust timing and starter quantity, and what to do when your bulk ferment stalls.

## Why cold kitchens wreck your timing

Yeast and lactic acid bacteria are temperature-sensitive in a non-linear way. A useful rule of thumb: every 1°C drop slows fermentation by roughly 8–10%. The math compounds fast.

- **26°C** — fast, often too fast for flavor development
- **24°C** — the "textbook" sourdough temperature most recipes assume
- **21°C** — comfortable, predictable, slightly slower
- **18°C** — bulk ferment may take 8–10 hours instead of 4–5
- **15°C** — fermentation crawls; expect 12+ hours or a stalled dough

The other shift is microbial. Cold favors lactic acid bacteria over yeast, so cold-fermented doughs tend to taste tangier with less rise. This is why fridge retards work for flavor but you can't bulk-ferment a dough at 6°C and expect it to puff up.

The single most important number in winter baking is the **dough temperature** — not the room temperature, not the water temperature. If you're not measuring it with a probe thermometer right after mixing, start there. Target 24–26°C for the final dough temperature (FDT) in winter, and you've already solved half the problem.

## Hitting the right dough temperature

Final dough temperature depends mostly on water temperature in home settings. A rough formula:

**Water temp = (Desired FDT × 3) − flour temp − room temp − friction factor**

Friction factor is around 2–3°C for hand mixing, 5–8°C for a stand mixer. So if your flour and kitchen are both at 17°C and you want a 25°C dough mixed by hand:

Water = (25 × 3) − 17 − 17 − 2 = **39°C**

That feels uncomfortably warm on your hand, but it's correct. Don't go above 40°C or you risk killing yeast on contact. If the calculator on this page is set up for it, plug in your numbers — it's faster than the arithmetic and easier to adjust on the fly.

## Where to find warmth in a cold house

You don't need a proofing box. You need a microclimate that holds 24–26°C reliably.

### The oven light trick

In most ovens, the interior light alone produces 24–28°C after 30 minutes with the door closed. Test yours: stick a thermometer in there with the light on for an hour and read the result. If it overshoots, prop the door open with a wooden spoon. If it undershoots, add a mug of just-boiled water for humidity and a few extra degrees.

### Other warm spots worth checking

- On top of the fridge (compressor heat) — usually 22–25°C
- Near (not on) a radiator — measure first, radiators surface at 60°C+
- Inside an insulated cooler with a jar of warm water swapped every few hours
- On top of a router or cable box — genuinely works, around 28–32°C
- Inside a microwave (off) with a mug of hot water

### Build a cheap proofer

A small seed-starting heat mat (around $15) plus a plastic tub gives you a stable 25–27°C environment. Put the mat under the tub, the dough inside, lid on. This is the closest thing to professional proofing you'll get at home for under $20.

## Adjusting your starter for winter

A sluggish starter is the most common winter complaint. Three changes help:

**Feed warmer water.** If you've been using room-temperature water, switch to 30–32°C. This alone often revives a starter that "won't peak."

**Increase the feeding ratio temporarily.** If you normally feed 1:5:5 (starter:flour:water), drop to 1:3:3 in winter so the starter peaks in a reasonable timeframe. Use it at peak — not 12 hours after peak when it's already collapsed and acidic.

**Keep it in a warm spot between feeds.** Same locations as above — oven with light on, top of the fridge, near (not on) a heat source.

If your starter is taking more than 8 hours to peak after feeding, it's too cold. Period. Move it.

## Adjusting bulk fermentation

This is where most winter loaves go wrong. Bakers follow a 4-hour recipe in a 17°C kitchen, shape underproofed dough, and pull a brick out of the oven.

Stop watching the clock. Watch the dough.

Signs bulk is done, regardless of how long it took:

- Volume increase of 50–75% (not double — that's overproofed for most home recipes)
- Domed surface with some bubbles visible on top and sides
- Jiggly when you shake the container
- Pulls away from the sides of the bowl when tilted

In a cold kitchen, expect bulk to take 6–10 hours instead of 4–5. If you can't commit to that, use a warm spot. Trying to rush bulk ferment in the cold is the single most common reason for dense winter loaves.

### Cold bulk strategy: stretch and folds matter more

When fermentation is slow, gluten development from time alone is also slower. Do 3–4 sets of stretch-and-folds at 30-minute intervals during the first 2 hours, then leave the dough alone. This builds structure without relying on enzymatic activity that's also crawling.

## Cold retard considerations

Winter doesn't change retard logic much, but a few notes:

- Your fridge is still 4°C. The kitchen doesn't change that.
- If your shaped dough was underproofed going in (common in winter), it won't catch up much in the fridge. Aim for 80% proofed before retarding.
- Some bakers skip the retard entirely in winter and bake straight from a long ambient cold bulk. The flavor profile is similar.

## A practical winter schedule

For a 17–18°C kitchen, a workable timeline:

- **8:00 AM** — Feed starter 1:3:3 with 32°C water, place in oven with light on
- **2:00 PM** — Starter at peak. Mix dough with warm water (target 25°C FDT)
- **2:30–4:30 PM** — Three sets of stretch-and-folds at 30-minute intervals, dough in warm spot
- **4:30–9:00 PM** — Bulk continues until 50–75% rise (watch the dough, not the clock)
- **9:00 PM** — Shape, into banneton, into fridge
- **Next morning** — Bake cold from the fridge

If your kitchen is colder than 17°C, extend bulk and consider moving the dough to a heated environment for the entire ferment.

## Common questions

### Why is my starter not doubling in winter?

It's too cold. A starter at 17°C might take 12+ hours to peak and could collapse before you notice. Move it to a 24–26°C spot — oven with light on, top of the fridge, or a heat mat. Feed with 30°C water. If it still doesn't double within 6–8 hours after that, the starter itself needs rebuilding with more frequent feeds for a few days.

### Can I bulk ferment overnight at room temperature in winter?

In a 15–18°C kitchen, yes — a long, slow overnight bulk of 10–14 hours can work and produces excellent flavor. Use less starter (5–10% of flour weight instead of 20%) so it doesn't overproof. Above 19°C overnight, you're risking an overproofed dough by morning.

### Should I use more starter in winter to speed things up?

You can, but it's a tradeoff. More starter means faster fermentation but less time for flavor development and less complex acidity. A better approach is to keep the starter percentage normal (15–20%) and raise the dough temperature instead. Only bump starter quantity if you can't access a warm spot.

### My dough is cold after mixing — can I warm it up?

Yes. Put the bowl in a slightly warm water bath (around 30°C) for 20–30 minutes to bring dough temperature up. Don't go hotter — you'll cook the outside of the dough. Check FDT again before deciding it's fixed.

### Is winter sourdough actually better-tasting?

Often, yes — if you let the slowness work for you. Long, cool fermentations build more complex acid profiles and better flavor. The mistake is fighting the cold instead of either embracing the long timeline or properly warming the dough. Pick one.

### How do I know if my loaf was underproofed vs. just dense from being cold?

Underproofed loaves have a tight, gummy crumb with large irregular tunnels near the top crust (the dough sprang up but had nothing to support the air pockets). They also tear rather than slice cleanly. Cold-fermented but properly proofed loaves have an even, slightly tighter crumb than summer loaves — that's normal and not a flaw.

## What to do today

1. Put a thermometer in your kitchen and read it at the time you usually mix dough. Write the number down.
2. Test your oven-light temperature for one hour with the door closed.
3. The next time you feed your starter, use 30°C water and place it in your warmest verified spot.
4. On your next bake, measure final dough temperature within 2 minutes of mixing. If it's below 24°C, adjust water temperature next time using the calculator above.
5. Stop watching the clock during bulk. Use volume and feel.

Cold kitchen sourdough is mostly a measurement problem disguised as a fermentation problem. Once you know the actual numbers — kitchen temp, dough temp, starter peak time — winter baking becomes predictable instead of frustrating.
