# Why is my sourdough gummy inside (and how to fix it)

You cut into the loaf, the crust crackles, the crumb looks beautiful — and then the bottom centimetre is wet, sticky, almost paste-like. That's gummy crumb. It's the most common sourdough failure mode, and the reason isn't always the one bakers expect.

## What "gummy" actually means

Gummy crumb is dough that hasn't fully transitioned from gel to bread. The starches haven't fully gelatinised, or they have but the structure trapping the steam wasn't strong enough to hold gas through the bake. The result is a damp, dense, doughy strip — usually at the bottom of the loaf where heat takes longest to penetrate.

It is not a hydration problem. Bread at 85% hydration can have a perfect open crumb. Bread at 70% hydration can be gummy. Hydration is rarely the cause.

## Cause #1: underproofed dough (the most common)

If your bulk fermentation was too short, the dough enters the oven without enough gas in the structure. It overheats from the outside in before the centre can bake. The crust forms, the crumb behind it doesn't, and you get gummy texture.

How to tell: a dense, tight crumb with little open structure. Few or no large alveoli. The loaf may look pale on top.

How to fix: extend bulk by 1–2 hours, or raise the inoculation by 5%. Track rise visually — aim for 65–75% volume increase from the moment salt was added, not from the start of mixing. The [bulk fermentation calculator](#schedule) can predict the right window for your kitchen temperature.

## Cause #2: overproofed dough (common in summer)

Counterintuitively, dough that's *too* fermented also bakes gummy. When bulk runs long, the gluten structure breaks down, the dough can't hold gas, and oven spring fails. The loaf flattens during baking, leaving a dense, sticky crumb.

How to tell: a flat loaf with little or no oven spring. Big bubbles right under the crust ("flying crust") and dense gummy crumb below them.

How to fix: shorten bulk by 1–2 hours, drop inoculation 5%, or move the dough to a cooler spot during bulk. In summer, watch the kitchen temperature — every 8°C above 24°C cuts bulk roughly in half.

## Cause #3: cut too early (zero-effort fix)

A loaf cut while still warm reads as gummy because steam hasn't escaped the crumb. The inside is still hot enough that water is condensed in the crumb structure. Wait at least an hour after the loaf comes out of the oven — the same loaf, cut at room temperature, will read as soft and bouncy instead of gummy.

This is the easiest fix in baking. Set a timer. Walk away.

## Cause #4: underbaked (verify with a thermometer)

A loaf that comes out at the *visual* sign of doneness (deep colour, hollow knock) is sometimes still underbaked internally. Sourdough crumb needs an internal temperature of **96–99°C (205–210°F)** to be fully set.

If you've never measured, do this once: stick an instant-read thermometer through the bottom of the loaf into the centre when you take it out. If it reads under 96°C, give it 5–10 more minutes in the oven, lid off, and use that data to calibrate your bake going forward.

## Cause #5: insufficient steam (less common)

If the crust sets too early, the loaf can't expand and the inside compacts. This is rare with a Dutch oven (which traps its own steam) but happens often with open baking on a stone if no steam source is provided. Symptoms: pale, soft, almost matte crust with dense gummy interior.

Fix: bake covered for the first 20 minutes (Dutch oven lid on, or a roasting pan inverted over the loaf), then uncover for browning.

## Cause #6: the recipe is wrong for the flour

Whole-grain flours absorb more water and ferment faster. If you took a 75% hydration recipe written for bread flour and made it with 50% whole wheat at the same hydration, you'll often get gummy results because there isn't enough water to fully hydrate the bran, and the bulk runs faster than the schedule predicts.

For 30%+ whole grain, raise hydration by 5–8% and shorten bulk by 15–20%.

## A diagnostic checklist

Before changing your recipe, run through this list in order:

1. **Did you cut into it warm?** If yes, retest with a fully cooled loaf next time.
2. **What was the internal temperature at bake-out?** If under 96°C, bake longer.
3. **What did the bulk look like — was the dough dense and tight, or flat and big-bubbled?** Underproofed vs overproofed have different fixes.
4. **What was your room temperature during bulk?** Plug it into the schedule calculator and compare predicted vs actual bulk duration.
5. **Did you bake covered?** If not, try a Dutch oven for 20 minutes covered then 25 uncovered.
6. **What flour did you use?** Whole-grain blends need their own recipe, not white-flour numbers with WW substituted.

Most gummy loaves trace back to causes 1, 2, or 3. Fix those first before chasing exotic explanations.

## When to change one variable at a time

The temptation after a gummy loaf is to change three things at once: longer bulk, higher temperature, more steam. Don't. Change one variable, bake again, and see what moved. You'll learn faster, and you'll have a stable recipe in 3–4 bakes instead of 15.
