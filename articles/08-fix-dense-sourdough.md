# How to fix dense sourdough (12 causes diagnosed)

Dense sourdough is the most common complaint from home bakers, and it's also one of the most misdiagnosed. Bakers add hydration thinking it will open the crumb. Or they buy a more expensive flour. Or they extend bulk by thirty minutes hoping it helps. Most of those changes don't fix the problem because the problem is rarely what they think it is.

Here's a real diagnostic walkthrough — twelve possible causes ranked by how often they actually explain dense crumb.

## 1. Underproofed bulk (the cause 70% of the time)

If your loaf comes out dense, this is the first place to look. Bulk fermentation that's too short leaves the dough without enough gas in the gluten structure. The dough enters the oven dense and stays dense.

Symptoms: tight uniform crumb, small even bubbles (or no visible bubbles), dough that felt firm and unyielding when you shaped it.

Fix: extend bulk by 1–2 hours, or raise inoculation by 5%. Track rise visually — aim for 65–75% volume increase from the moment salt was added. The bulk fermentation calculator on this site predicts the right window for your kitchen temperature.

## 2. Bulk at too low a temperature

Same effect as cause #1, but the diagnostic is different. Maybe your bulk lasted 6 hours like the recipe said, but your kitchen was 18 °C and the recipe was written for 24 °C. At 18 °C, that 6-hour bulk is the equivalent of a 3-hour bulk at 24 °C. Massively underproofed.

Fix: measure dough temperature, not room temperature. A 22 °C kitchen often means 24 °C dough due to mixing friction. Use the actual dough temperature in the calculator and trust the new bulk prediction.

## 3. Weak starter

A starter that hasn't peaked or is past peak adds too few yeast cells to the dough. Bulk runs slowly, the dough never quite gets there, and you bake an underproofed loaf.

Symptoms: bulk that "should" be done by all timing rules but visually hasn't moved much. Dough feels dense throughout fermentation.

Fix: refresh the starter more often before mixing, use it within 1–2 hours of peak (not 6 hours), and run a 3-feed revival if needed. The Starter tab on this site calculates feed timing backwards from mix time.

## 4. Wrong flour

Bread flour and all-purpose flour are not interchangeable for sourdough. AP at 10–11% protein cannot hold the gas required for an open crumb. The crumb will be tight and dense regardless of how perfect the rest of your technique is.

Fix: use bread flour with 12–13% protein minimum. King Arthur Bread Flour, Caputo "1," T80 in Europe — anything labelled "bread flour" or "high protein."

## 5. Hydration too low

A 65% dough is naturally denser than a 75% dough. If you're chasing an open crumb at 65%, you'll never get there. The water isn't there to create the alveolar structure.

Fix: raise hydration to 72–78% for a baseline open-crumb country loaf. Whole grain doughs need 5–8% more.

## 6. Skipped or weak folds

Folds (stretch-and-fold or coil folds) develop gluten and align the gluten network. Without them, especially at higher hydrations, the dough never builds structure to hold gas.

Fix: 4 sets of folds during the first 2 hours of bulk. Strong folds in sets 1–2, gentle folds in sets 3–4.

## 7. Pre-shape too tight

A tight pre-shape squeezes gas out of the dough and into a small volume. The gluten network doesn't recover during the brief bench rest, and the loaf never expands properly in the oven.

Fix: pre-shape with a light touch — round the dough loosely, just enough to give it a smooth top.

## 8. Final shape too tight or too loose

Too tight crushes the gas structure built during bulk. Too loose lets the loaf spread instead of expanding upward, producing a flatter, denser loaf.

Fix: aim for a shape that holds form on the counter for 30 seconds without flattening. The dough should feel taut but not strained.

## 9. Cold Dutch oven

Sourdough oven spring depends on a hot Dutch oven. A cold Dutch oven (preheated only 20 minutes, or not at all) doesn't deliver the temperature shock that drives expansion. You'll get a dense loaf even when everything else was right.

Fix: preheat the empty Dutch oven for 60 minutes at 250 °C before baking. Set a timer.

## 10. No steam in the oven

If you bake on an open stone instead of a Dutch oven, the lack of steam lets the crust set early. The loaf can't expand inside the cement-like crust, and you get a dense crumb.

Fix: cover the loaf with an inverted roasting pan for the first 20 minutes, or put a tray of boiling water on the bottom rack. Or switch to a Dutch oven, which traps its own steam.

## 11. Too cold from retard, then too long out

Some bakers retard their dough then leave it at room temperature for an hour before baking, thinking it needs to "warm up." It doesn't. Cold dough drives more oven spring than warm dough — the temperature differential matters. A retarded dough left out for 60 minutes loses that advantage and bakes denser.

Fix: take the dough straight from the fridge to the hot Dutch oven. Score, transfer, lid on. Done.

## 12. Underbaked (genuinely uncommon but happens)

Bread that comes out at the visual sign of doneness (deep colour, hollow knock) is sometimes still underbaked. The crumb hasn't fully gelatinised, and what reads as "dense" is actually "doughy."

Fix: probe the loaf at bake-out. Internal temperature should be 96–99 °C. If under, give it 5–10 more minutes lid off.

## The diagnostic order

If your bread is dense, work this list top to bottom:

1. Did the dough rise visibly during bulk? (If no → starter problem)
2. What was the dough temperature during bulk? (Not the room — the dough)
3. What flour did you use? (Bread flour minimum)
4. What was hydration? (72%+ for open crumb)
5. Did you do 4 folds? (Or did you skip after one?)
6. How was the pre-shape and final shape tension?
7. Was the Dutch oven preheated 60 min?
8. Did you score and bake covered for 20 min?

Most dense loaves are explained by causes 1, 2, or 4. Fix those before chasing exotic explanations.

## What to change between bakes

Change one variable at a time. If you raise hydration AND extend bulk AND switch flour all at once, you won't know which fix worked. Pick the most likely cause from the list above, change only that, bake again, observe the result. You'll have a stable open-crumb recipe in 3–4 bakes instead of 15 frustrated attempts.
