# Bulk fermentation by temperature: a baker's guide

Bulk fermentation is the part of a sourdough bake where most outcomes are decided. Get the timing right and you get an open crumb, a clean ear, and dough that's a pleasure to shape. Get it wrong and the rest of the schedule cannot save you. The single biggest variable that decides bulk timing isn't your starter, your flour, or your inoculation — it's your kitchen temperature.

## The Q10 rule, in plain words

Yeast and lactic bacteria work faster when it's warm and slower when it's cold. The rate roughly doubles for every 8°C (about 14°F) rise in dough temperature. Bakers call this Q10 ≈ 2.

What this means in practice:

- At **24°C (75°F)** a 20% inoculation white-flour dough finishes bulk in about **5 hours**.
- At **20°C (68°F)** the same dough takes about **7.5 hours**.
- At **16°C (61°F)** it takes nearly **11 hours**.
- At **28°C (82°F)** it's done in about **3 hours and 20 minutes**.

The dough you mixed at 9 a.m. on a 22°C summer Saturday will be ready around 3 p.m. The same dough mixed at 9 a.m. on a 17°C winter Saturday will be ready around 7 p.m. Treating those two days as if they were the same bake is the most common reason home loaves come out underproofed in winter and overproofed in summer.

## Measure dough temperature, not air temperature

Dough is usually warmer than the room. Friction from mixing adds 1–4°C, and the dough then loses heat slowly because flour and water hold heat well. If your kitchen is 22°C, your dough may be sitting at 24–25°C right after mixing. Plan for the dough's temperature, not the thermostat.

A cheap instant-read probe thermometer is enough. Stick it in the centre of the dough right after mixing. That number is what you should feed into the [schedule calculator](#schedule).

## Inoculation as a second dial

After temperature, inoculation (the percentage of starter relative to total flour) is your other lever. More starter speeds bulk down; less starter slows it.

- **10–15% inoculation** — slower bulk, often 8–14 hours at room temp. Good for summer or for letting bulk run overnight in winter.
- **20% inoculation** — the most common starting point. Predictable.
- **25–30% inoculation** — faster bulk, sometimes 3 hours or less in a warm kitchen. Useful for same-day bakes.

The relationship is roughly linear. Halving the inoculation roughly doubles the bulk time. Doubling the inoculation roughly halves it.

## Visual cues beat the clock

A schedule is a planning tool, not a stopwatch. The dough finishes bulk when it does, not when the timer says. Look for:

- **50–75% rise** in volume from the moment salt was added. Mark the side of the bowl with a wax pencil or a piece of tape so you can see this clearly.
- **A domed top** that holds its shape when you tilt the bowl gently.
- **A jiggle** — when you wobble the container, the surface moves like a thick custard, not like wet concrete.
- **Bubbles visible at the surface and the sides** of the container.

If two of those four are present, you're at or near the end of bulk.

## How to use the calculator with your kitchen

Open the schedule calculator, set inoculation, hydration, and salt to your recipe. Then set the room-temperature slider to your *measured* room temperature, not the value on your thermostat. The calculator uses the Q10 model above to estimate bulk duration, then schedules everything else around it — autolyse, folds, pre-shape, retard, and bake-out.

If you find your bulk consistently runs faster than predicted, your dough is probably warmer than the room (heating from the floor, sun on the counter, oven lights, etc.). Drop the room slider 1–2°C and the prediction will line up.

## What changes the model can't see

The calculator assumes a healthy starter peaking on schedule. If your starter is sluggish, off-schedule, or over-fed, no temperature math fixes that — it adds latency at the front. The Starter tab in the same tool plans those feeds backwards from mix time so your levain actually peaks when you need it.

The model also assumes white bread flour. Whole-grain flours ferment faster — they bring more enzymes and more wild yeast on the bran. If you're running 50% whole wheat, expect to shave 20–30% off the predicted bulk time, and watch the dough closely.

## A starting recipe for calibration

If you've never measured before, run this once: 500 g bread flour, 75% hydration (375 g water), 20% inoculation (100 g starter), 2% salt (10 g). Mix at a known time. Note the dough temperature. Bulk in a clear container with a marked starting line. Note the time when the dough hits the 65% rise mark.

That single observation calibrates the model to your kitchen forever. You'll find your bulks are within ±20 minutes of the predicted value from then on, regardless of season — as long as you measure dough temperature, not room temperature.
