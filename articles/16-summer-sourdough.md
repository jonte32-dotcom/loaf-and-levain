# Summer sourdough: don't let the dough run away

Your kitchen hits 82°F and suddenly the bulk ferment you nailed all winter turns into a slack, over-proofed puddle by the time you go to shape it. This is the summer sourdough problem, and almost every home baker runs into it once the seasons shift.

The fix isn't complicated, but it requires letting go of the rigid recipe times you've been following. In summer, the dough sets the pace, not the clock.

## Why summer changes everything

Yeast activity roughly doubles for every 17°F (10°C) increase in dough temperature, within the range we care about. A dough at 78°F ferments noticeably faster than one at 72°F. At 82°F it's flying. Lactic acid bacteria also shift their behavior — warmer doughs tend to produce more acetic acid early and more lactic acid as fermentation drags on, which changes flavor as well as timing.

Two things go wrong in summer:

1. **Bulk fermentation overshoots.** You follow a 5-hour bulk that worked in March, and by hour 3 the dough is already 90% risen, gassy, and sliding off the bench scraper.
2. **The dough heats up during bulk.** Mixing generates friction. A warm kitchen heats the dough further. By the end of bulk, dough that started at 76°F can be 82°F+ — and the last hour ferments at the fastest rate.

Both problems compound. The result is open-crumb dough that turns into a flat, sour, gummy loaf.

## The single most important number: dough temperature

If you don't already track desired dough temperature (DDT), summer is when you start. Most sourdough recipes assume a DDT around 76–78°F. In a hot kitchen, you have to actively cool your inputs to hit that.

The formula for a no-knead or low-mix dough:

**Water temp = (DDT × 3) − (flour temp + room temp + starter temp)**

Example: kitchen 82°F, flour 80°F, starter 78°F, DDT 76°F.
Water temp = (76 × 3) − (80 + 78 + 82) = 228 − 240 = **−12°F**.

That's impossible, which tells you something important: in a hot kitchen, water temperature alone can't save you. You need to either chill the flour, cool the starter, or accept a higher DDT and shorten bulk drastically.

The [dough temperature calculator on this page](#) handles the arithmetic and accounts for friction factor if you machine-mix. Use it before you start, not after.

### Practical ways to cool your inputs

- **Refrigerate flour** for a few hours before mixing. A bag of flour in the fridge overnight can drop 20°F.
- **Use ice water.** Weigh ice as part of your hydration total. 50g ice + 300g cold water = 350g water for the recipe.
- **Use a cool starter.** A starter pulled straight from a recent feed at room temp is warm and active. Some bakers feed earlier and refrigerate for 30 minutes before mixing.
- **Mix in a cool spot.** Granite or stone counters pull heat out of the bowl.

## Adjusting bulk fermentation for heat

Forget "bulk for 4–5 hours." Watch the dough.

Here are realistic bulk times at common dough temperatures, assuming roughly 20% starter and a healthy levain:

| Dough temp | Approx. bulk time | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| 72°F | 6–8 hours | 50% rise, smooth top, jiggly |
| 76°F | 4.5–6 hours | 50% rise, domed, bubbles on sides |
| 78°F | 3.5–4.5 hours | 40–50% rise |
| 80°F | 3–3.5 hours | 30–40% rise, faster surface bubbles |
| 82°F+ | 2–3 hours | 25–35% rise — check often |

The hotter the dough, the **less** rise you want before shaping. That sounds counterintuitive, but warm dough keeps fermenting aggressively after you shape it, during the proof, and even into the early bake. You're banking on residual activity.

### Signs of bulk completion in hot weather

- Dough has risen 25–50% (lower end if warm)
- Surface is domed and shiny, not flat
- Visible bubbles on the sides of the container
- Jiggles like set custard when nudged
- Pulls away cleanly from the container

If you see large surface bubbles that have already popped, or the dough looks slack and webby, you're past the window. Shape immediately and get it into the fridge.

## Cold retard: your summer best friend

A cold retard (overnight in the fridge after shaping) does two important things in summer: it slows fermentation to a manageable rate, and it firms the dough so scoring works.

In hot weather, **shape earlier than you think and retard longer**. A 12–16 hour cold proof at 38–40°F gives you flavor without over-proofing. Some bakers shape after just a short bulk (sometimes only 2 hours at 82°F) and let the bulk finish in the fridge during the early hours of retard, before the dough cools below ~50°F where activity nearly stops.

If your fridge runs warm (above 42°F), your retard isn't really retarding. Buy a fridge thermometer and check.

## Hydration: dial it back

If you normally bake at 78% hydration, drop to 72–75% in summer. Two reasons:

1. Warm dough is structurally weaker. Gluten softens at higher temperatures, and the dough holds shape worse.
2. Faster fermentation means more enzymatic activity, which degrades gluten further.

A slightly stiffer dough gives you a margin of error. You can always push hydration back up in October.

## Starter management when it's hot

Your starter is in the same hot kitchen. It will peak fast — sometimes in 3–4 hours instead of 6–8 — and collapse before you're ready to mix.

Options:

- **Feed at lower ratios in reverse.** Use a 1:5:5 or 1:10:10 feed (starter:flour:water) so it takes longer to peak.
- **Use cool water** for starter feeds. 60–65°F water slows things down.
- **Build a levain on a timer.** If you want to mix at 9am, work backward. At 80°F room temp, a 1:5:5 levain might peak in 5 hours. Mix the levain at 4am — or use a smaller ratio so it peaks later.
- **Keep the mother in the fridge** between bakes and only build levains for active baking. This is what most experienced bakers do year-round and it pays off doubly in summer.

## Shaping and scoring a warm dough

Warm dough is slack dough. Two adjustments:

- **Use more flour on the bench** during shaping than you would in winter. Light, but use it.
- **Pre-shape, rest 15–20 minutes, then final shape.** The rest lets surface tension build. Skipping pre-shape works in winter but often fails in summer.
- **Refrigerate before scoring.** Warm dough drags on the lazme. A loaf that's been in the fridge 8+ hours scores cleanly.

## A summer-adjusted timeline

Here's a workable schedule for a kitchen running 80°F:

| Time | Step |
|---|---|
| 7:00 am | Feed levain 1:5:5 with 65°F water |
| 12:00 pm | Levain peaks; mix dough with ice water targeting 76°F DDT |
| 12:30 pm | Stretch and fold #1 |
| 1:00 pm | Stretch and fold #2 |
| 1:45 pm | Coil fold |
| 3:30–4:00 pm | Bulk complete at ~35% rise; pre-shape |
| 4:15 pm | Final shape, into banneton |
| 4:30 pm | Into fridge |
| Next morning, 7:00 am | Bake straight from fridge |

The total bulk window here is only about 3.5 hours. In winter, the same flour and starter would need 5.5 hours. Same dough, different season.

## Common questions

### How do I know if my dough is over-proofed before I bake it?

Poke test: lightly flour a finger, press 1/2 inch into the dough. If it springs back fully, under-proofed. If it springs back slowly and leaves a slight dent, ready. If it doesn't spring back at all, or the dough deflates, over-proofed. In summer, an over-proofed dough often also looks visibly bubbly on the surface and feels almost liquid underneath.

### Can I just bulk ferment in the fridge in summer?

You can, but pure cold bulk ferments are slow and tend to produce sour, dense loaves with tight crumb because the yeast slows down faster than the bacteria. A better approach: 1–2 hours of warm bulk to get fermentation started, then move the container to the fridge for 8–12 hours, then shape cold and proof in the fridge.

### My starter doubles in 3 hours now. Is that bad?

No — that's normal for warm temperatures and means it's healthy. But it changes how you build levains. Don't feed a starter, walk away for 8 hours, and expect to use it. Either feed smaller ratios (1:10:10), use cool water, or refrigerate after it peaks.

### Should I change my flour blend in summer?

A small bump in whole grain (5–10% more whole wheat or rye) ferments faster, which doesn't help you. If anything, lean a bit more on bread flour and white flour blends in summer for better gluten structure in warm conditions. Save the whole-grain experiments for spring and fall.

### Why does my summer crumb look tight even though the dough seemed well-proofed?

Usually because the dough over-proofed during bulk, lost gas structure, and then couldn't recover during the bake. Counterintuitively, tight, gummy crumb in summer is often an over-proofing problem, not under-proofing. Shorten your bulk by 30–45 minutes and see what happens.

### Do I need a proofing box or special equipment?

No. Most summer issues are solved with a thermometer (cheap instant-read), ice, and a willingness to ignore the recipe clock. A proofing box matters more in winter when you need to add heat than in summer when you're trying to remove it.

## Start here today

1. Take your kitchen's temperature right now. Take your flour's temperature.
2. Plug those into the dough temperature calculator above and find your water temp for a 76°F DDT.
3. On your next bake, cut your usual bulk time by 30% and judge by rise and feel, not by the clock.
4. Shape earlier, retard longer, and bake straight from the fridge.

Summer baking isn't worse than winter baking — the loaves can be more flavorful because of the active fermentation. But the margin for error shrinks. Watch the dough, trust your thermometer, and stop running the same timeline you used in February.
