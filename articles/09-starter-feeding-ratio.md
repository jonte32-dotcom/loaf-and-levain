# Starter feeding ratios: 1:1:1 vs 1:2:2 vs 1:5:5

The colon notation describes a feed: starter to flour to water by weight. A 1:2:2 feed at 20 g of starter means 20 g starter + 40 g flour + 40 g water. The ratio you choose changes how long the starter takes to peak and how strong it is when it does. Most home bakers use one ratio for everything, but matching ratio to schedule unlocks more control over your bake.

## What changes as the ratio gets bigger

A 1:1:1 feed has a high starter concentration. The yeast and bacteria from the previous feed dominate the substrate. They eat through the available sugars fast and peak quickly.

A 1:5:5 feed has a low starter concentration. The same population now has 5 times more food to work through. They peak slowly, but the population grows substantially before they run out. The peak is bigger, the starter is stronger, and the time to peak is much longer.

In between, 1:2:2 sits at the sweet spot for most home bakers — peaks in a manageable window, produces enough starter to bake with, and gives enough food that the starter doesn't crash within hours.

## Time to peak at typical kitchen temperatures

These are approximate, calibrated for a healthy starter at 22 °C kitchen temperature. Cooler kitchens add time; warmer kitchens cut it.

| Ratio | Peak time at 22 °C | Use case |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1:1 | 3–4 h | Refresh feed before a build feed; quick maintenance |
| 1:2:2 | 5–6 h | Standard pre-bake feed; the home baker default |
| 1:3:3 | 7–8 h | Overnight peak (feed in evening, bake midday) |
| 1:5:5 | 8–10 h | Long overnight; or for low-acid starter |
| 1:10:10 | 12–16 h | Very weak starter food; less common |

Halve the kitchen temperature relative to the rule and roughly double these times. At 16 °C, a 1:2:2 takes 10–12 hours instead of 5–6.

## When to use a 1:1:1

This is a tight feed. Use it when:

- **You need to bake same-day and don't have time to wait 8 hours.** A 1:1:1 in the morning peaks by lunch.
- **The starter has been weak.** Concentration feeds (1:1:1 or even 1:0.5:0.5) help rebuild yeast population from a sluggish starter.
- **As a refresh feed before a build feed.** When you have a starter that's been in the fridge, a 1:1:1 wakes it up before a 1:2:2 builds the volume you need.

The downside: 1:1:1 starters peak fast and crash fast. If you miss the window by an hour, you're using sliding starter, which produces underproofed dough.

## When to use a 1:2:2

The default. Use it when:

- **You feed the night before for a morning bake** — peaks 5–6 hours after feeding, sits at near-peak for another hour or two.
- **You feed in the morning for an evening bake** — same window.
- **Your kitchen is at typical room temperature** (20–24 °C).
- **You don't have a strong reason to use a different ratio.**

Most published sourdough recipes assume a 1:2:2 feed unless they say otherwise.

## When to use a 1:5:5 or higher

A loose feed. Use it when:

- **You want a long peak window** — 8–10 hours. Useful for overnight feeds where you want peak to coincide with a morning mix.
- **The starter has been over-acidic.** A 1:5:5 dilutes accumulated acids and produces a milder, sweeter starter.
- **You want maximum yeast strength.** Bigger food supply means more yeast cells produced before crash.
- **You're storing the starter at room temp between bakes** — bigger feeds last longer between feeds.

The downside: longer wait times. If you misjudge timing, you can be waiting hours for peak.

## Why peak timing beats average timing

The 1-hour window centred on peak is when starter is at maximum yeast cell count and minimum bacterial dominance. A starter used 2 hours past peak ferments the dough more slowly (yeast cells starting to die back) and produces a more acidic loaf. A starter used 2 hours before peak doesn't have enough yeast biomass yet — bulk will run long.

## Multi-feed building

For a starter coming out of the fridge after a week or more, one feed isn't enough. The yeast population is depressed and a single feed produces a peak too weak for reliable baking.

The standard 2-feed protocol:

1. **Refresh feed (1:1:1 or 1:0.5:0.5):** small ratio, concentrates the surviving population. Peak in 4–6 hours.
2. **Build feed (1:2:2):** at the moment refresh peaks, build up to the volume you need for mixing. Peak in 5–6 hours.

Total: 9–12 hours from fridge to bakeable starter. The Starter tab schedules this automatically.

For very weak or fridge-forgotten starters (>3 weeks), use a 3-feed protocol:

1. **Wake feed (1:1:1, small):** wake up dormant population. 8–12 hours.
2. **Refresh feed (1:1:1 or 1:2:2):** build population. 5–6 hours.
3. **Build feed (1:2:2):** target volume. 5–6 hours.

Total: 18–24 hours. Patient, but reliable.

## Quick rule of thumb

If you want to remember just one thing: **1:2:2 covers 90% of bakes.** Use it as your default. Only switch to 1:1:1 when you're in a hurry, or 1:5:5 when you want a slow, big peak.

A starter you understand at one ratio is more useful than a starter you've never timed correctly across three.
